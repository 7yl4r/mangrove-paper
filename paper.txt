Highlights
What are the main findings?
High spatial resolution (~2 m pixels) satellite-derived annual maps show that 2017 hurricanes caused extensive mangrove disturbance, with only 46% and 59% of pre-storm mangrove area remaining after one year in Rookery Bay and Jobos Bay, respectively; by 2023-2024, cover reached 90% and 83% of pre-storm extent, but recovery was much lower where disturbed pixels transitioned to coastal water. 
Satellite-derived Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) monthly time series at 10 m and higher temporal resolution showed rapid post-storm rebound within the first post-storm year followed by slower multi-year recovery in both reserves.
What are the implications of the main findings?
Extensive but largely reversible canopy loss shows that mangrove forests in both reserves are highly resilient, with most disturbed areas returning to mangrove cover within a few years, while low recovery where pixels transitioned to coastal water (coastal fringes) or with pre-existing stressors flags vulnerable areas at risk of persistent degradation.
NDVI results highlight that mangrove canopies quickly regain basic photosynthetic function after hurricanes but require several years to fully rebuild structure and condition, which is important for assessing recovery trajectories and timing post-storm monitoring.
Abstract
Two U.S. National Estuarine Research Reserves (NERRs), Rookery Bay (Florida) and Jobos Bay (Puerto Rico), were near major hurricanes in 2017 (Irma, Maria) and 2022 (Ian, Fiona). We quantified mangrove disturbance and recovery using pixel-wise annual land-cover trajectories from 2-m WorldView™ imagery (2017-2024) combined with monthly Sentinel-2 and Landsat Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) time series (2016-2025). One year after the 2017 storms, mangrove cover decreased to 46% (Rookery Bay) and 59% (Jobos Bay) of pre-storm extent, with most disturbed pixels transitioning to bare soil (45% and 27%, respectively). By 2020, mangrove cover recovered to 81% (Rookery) and 73% (Jobos), further reaching 90% (2023) and 83% (2024). Recovery was spatially heterogeneous: areas transitioning to low vegetation showed the highest recovery (92%/86% in Rookery/Jobos), followed by soil (86%/68%), while areas converted to coastal water showed the lowest recovery (48%/36%). Persistent degradation coincided with pre-existing hydrologic stressors or human development. Recovered mangroves exhibited a sharp post-storm NDVI drop followed by rapid initial rebound within one year and then by gradual recovery with reemerging seasonality through mid-2022. NDVI recovery began earlier in Jobos Bay (November 2017) than in southern Rookery Bay (June 2018), possibly associated with greater disturbance in the latter due to right-side hurricane exposure. No evident impacts were detected from the 2022 storms. The contrasting responses between 2017 (hurricane-force winds ≥64 kt at the NERRs) and 2022 (tropical-storm-force winds ≥50 kt at the NERRs) suggest local wind exposure was the primary driver of widespread canopy disturbance, whereas extreme rainfall and storm surge alone did not trigger comparable spectral changes. Integrating structural (annual maps) and functional (NDVI) indicators provides a transferable framework for assessing mangrove resilience and identifying persistent post-storm degradation.
Keywords: National Estuarine Research Reserves; Jobos Bay, Rookery Bay; coastal resilience; mangrove extent; land-cover maps; land-cover trajectories; NDVI; remote sensing; coastal resilience.


1. Introduction
Mangroves provide critical ecosystem services by stabilizing shorelines, reducing storm-surge impacts, and mitigating coastal flooding for coastal communities. Loss or degradation of coastal mangroves can significantly increase the land area exposed to damaging surge inundation [1]. Tropical cyclones are major disturbances to mangrove forests: strong winds, storm surge, and coastal erosion can uproot trees, defoliate canopies, and alter species composition and stand structure [2,3]. Severe storms can also modify hydrology, salinity, sediment deposition, and soil redox conditions, including sulfide concentrations, with consequences ranging from seedling mortality to widespread death of mature stands [2,4]. The effects of tropical cyclones can be cumulative, with wind, surge, and rainfall jointly influencing post-disturbance recovery conditions [5]. Many mangrove forests show rapid functional recovery of canopy leaf area and carbon exchange after major disturbances, returning to pre-storm conditions within a few years [3,6], although structural recovery can be slower and some stands remain degraded.
Satellite remote sensing provides a powerful tool for monitoring the distribution and area extent of mangrove ecosystems across large spatial scales. Time series of annual and seasonal mangrove mosaics have been used to quantify hurricane-related canopy damage and track recovery following storms [3,7–9]. Monitoring mangrove forests is valuable to managers seeking to conserve habitat quality and sustain ecosystem services [10]. In the United States, two National Estuarine Research Reserves (NERRs), namely Rookery Bay in southwest Florida, and Jobos Bay in south-central Puerto Rico (Figure 1), have coastlines dominated by mangrove habitats that are frequently exposed to tropical cyclones. In September 2017, Hurricane Irma passed over Rookery Bay, and Hurricane Maria passed very close to Jobos Bay, affecting mangroves in both reserves. In September 2022, Hurricanes Ian and Fiona again passed near both reserves (Figure 1). Collaborative research projects were implemented to characterize storm impacts on mangroves: “Resilience of the Mangrove Coast” [11] and “Limits of Resilience” [12], hereafter referred to as the NERRS Mangrove Coast Collective (NERRS-MCC). These projects generated a diverse set of products to improve understanding of long-term mangrove disturbance and recovery under repeated tropical-cyclone exposure [13,14]. Among the primary outputs of the NERRS-MCC projects was an annual time series (2014-2024) of high-resolution (2-m) land-cover maps derived from WorldViewTM imagery using a decision-tree classification [15,16]. 
The aim of this study was to characterize and quantify post-storm changes in mangrove cover using two complementary approaches: satellite-derived land-cover maps and spectral time series. We focused on mangrove resistance, recovery, and identifying areas that showed persistent degradation following the 2017 storms. Post-storm classification maps (2018) revealed widespread transitions from mangroves to other land-cover classes at both reserves, as canopy loss and downed trees exposed the underlying substrate [17]. We quantified pixel-wise land-cover change from 2017 to 2024 at Jobos Bay and from 2017 to 2023 in the southeastern portion of Rookery Bay. We also examined the time series (2016-2025) of the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) derived from Sentinel-2 and Landsat imagery at spatial resolution of 10 and 30 m pixels to characterize storm disturbance and post-storm recovery timing at finer (monthly means and single-date observations) temporal resolution.

2. Materials and Methods
2.1. Study Areas and Hurricane Disturbance
The study focused on the Rookery Bay and Jobos Bay National Estuarine Research Reserves (NERRs) and adjacent coastal ecosystems. Rookery Bay is in southwest Florida and Jobos Bay is located south-central Puerto Rico. Mangrove ecosystems in south Florida and Puerto Rico are composed of three true mangrove tree species: Avicennia germinans (black mangrove), Laguncularia racemosa (white mangrove), and Rhizophora mangle (red mangrove) [19]. Within the boundaries of Jobos Bay and Rookery Bay NERRs, mangrove ecosystems cover approximately 1,000 and 31,000 acres, respectively [20]. Mangrove structure in both reserves ranges from scrub forests composed of small individuals (<2 m) to taller, forested stands with trees typically 10–15 m in height. Mixed-species assemblages are common in both reserves, along with tidally-dominated fringing forests characterized primarily by red mangrove and lower-elevation basin forests dominated by black mangrove [21]. 
We focused our temporal sampling to capture mangrove cover and extent before and after four hurricanes that affected the study regions: Irma (2017) and Ian (2022) affected southwest Florida, and Maria (2017) and Fiona (2022) that impacted Puerto Rico. Storm exposure was characterized using National Hurricane Center best track and wind swath data [18] to document trajectories and wind fields affecting each reserve (Figure 1). 
During the September 2017 storms, both reserves experienced hurricane-force winds (≥64 kt; Figure 1a,b). Hurricane Irma made landfall near Marco Island, directly affecting the center of Rookery Bay NERR as a Category 3 storm with maximum sustained winds of about 100 kt, storm tides of 6-10 ft above ground level (AGL), and 10-15 in of rainfall across the southern portion of Rookery Bay [22]. Hurricane Maria made landfall about 40 km east of Jobos Bay NERR as a Category 4 storm with maximum sustained winds of 135 kt, generating storm tides of around 4 ft AGL along Jobos Bay and 7-10 in of rainfall in the area [23]. 
During the September 2022 storms, both reserves were exposed to strong tropical-storm-force winds (≥50 kt; Figure 1c,d) but remained outside the core band of hurricane-force wind fields. Hurricane Ian made landfall as a Category 4 storm approximately 100 km north of Rookery Bay, producing maximum inundation of about 7 ft AGL near Marco Island and 5-7 in of rainfall in the surrounding area [24]. Hurricane Fiona made landfall as a Category 1 storm approximately 100 km west of Jobos Bay, generating 1-3 ft of storm surge inundation AGL along the southern coast of Puerto Rico and heavy rainfall of 20-25 in around Jobos Bay [25]. These contrasting exposure conditions allow comparison of mangrove responses to severe (2017) versus less intense (2022) tropical-cyclone impacts and assess the effects of repeated disturbances. 
2.2. Annual Land-Cover Mapping and Recovery Analysis
NERRS-MCC produced a time series of 2-m annual land-cover maps spanning 2014-2024 for Rookery Bay and Jobos Bay NERRs and the surrounding ecosystems extending beyond the reserve boundaries (Figure 1, blue polygons). The maps used a decision-tree classifier developed by McCarthy et al. [15,16]. This hierarchical classifier assigns each pixel to a land-cover class through a sequence of binary decision rules and has been previously applied to mangrove mapping and hurricane-impact assessments [7,10]. This classifier identifies mangroves and exposed soil more accurately than support vector machine and neural network classifiers [10] and was successfully applied to detect mangrove damage following Hurricane Irma in Rookery Bay [7]. All technical and data reports, datasets, metadata, and data visualizations for both projects can be found in Office for Coastal Management reports [13,14]. 
For this study, the analysis of the land-cover maps was restricted to areas with consistent spatial coverage throughout the available time series and documented hurricane impacts, which included the southeastern region of Rookery Bay NERR (2017-2023, Figure A1) and the area surrounding Jobos Bay (2017-2024, Figure A2, and Figure 1c,d, red polygons). The southeastern region of Rookery Bay was the reserve area with more storm damage (Figure A1); however, due to lack of available satellite images for 2023, only the southernmost region of the reserve was used for this analysis (Figure 1a,b, red polygon). 
The 2017 maps were derived from imagery acquired prior to hurricane landfall in September (Table A1). This represents pre-storm conditions. Subsequent annual maps were used to quantify hurricane-induced disturbance and evaluate ecosystem recovery.
Satellite imagery and preprocessing: Habitat maps were generated from very-high-resolution (~2 m), 8‑band multispectral WorldView‑2 and WorldView‑3 imagery provided by the NASA Commercial Smallsat Data Acquisition Program [26]. Level‑1B images were orthorectified, atmospherically corrected, and converted to remote‑sensing reflectance following McCarthy et al. [16], using a Digital Elevation Model (Copernicus GLO-30; [27]) and standard GDAL/MATLAB workflows [28,29] on a high‑performance computing cluster. Residual georeferencing errors, particularly in Jobos Bay, were reduced by manually aligning reflectance images to high‑resolution coastline vectors from NOAA’s Continually Updated Shoreline Product (CUSP, [30]).  
Annual land-cover maps: Land-cover classification followed McCarthy et al. [15,16], using spectral indices and a decision-tree algorithm implemented in MATLAB without in situ training data. Classes were grouped into vegetated and non-vegetated types (Table A2), with mangroves distinguished from upland vegetation, low-relief vegetation, marsh, and unvegetated surfaces. Cloud and shadow pixels were explicitly classified, allowing partial use of cloud-contaminated scenes [15]. 
Annual mosaics were produced by combining classified scenes using the pixel-wise mode of class values using imagery acquired between January and April to minimize atmospheric variability; otherwise, the best available scenes were selected (Table A1). Cloud and shadow classes were merged into a no-data category in the annual composites (Table A2). Mosaicking was conducted in Google Earth Engine [31]. A complete list of image acquisitions and processing details is provided in the project Dataset Description [32].  
Map development incorporated iterative input from stakeholders at both reserves, including managers, advisory committees, and technical collaborators, to improve ecological realism and management relevance. Stakeholders contributed local ecological knowledge and management priorities to define regions of interest, refine classification rules, and review intermediate and final products. This process revealed georeferencing inconsistencies addressed by manual alignment, artifacts from the original maximum-value mosaicking approach [16] addressed by adopting mode-based compositing, and confusion between mangroves and upland vegetation in Jobos Bay addressed by adjusting decision-tree thresholds.
Post-processing combined temporal prevalence and spatial filters to reduce misclassifications. For each pixel, we computed the percentage of years assigned to each land-cover class and reclassified rare, context-inconsistent assignments (e.g., vegetation or mangrove classes occurring infrequently within pixels classified as water in most years) as no-data. For vegetation classes, prevalence criteria were combined with spatial masks derived from expert-defined polygons and existing habitat maps to indicate where mangroves were expected or unlikely to occur. Suspected mangrove-upland confusion was removed by reclassifying unlikely mangrove pixels outside known mangrove areas or with low mangrove prevalence as no-data. In Rookery Bay, pixels outside existing habitat maps were revised using temporal prevalence alone. Stakeholder input, obtained through repeated project meetings at both reserves, improved spatial consistency, classification accuracy, and ecological realism in the final land-cover time series.
Due to limited imagery, maps were not produced for either reserve in 2019. Availability of WorldViewTM images for Rookery Bay decreased after 2020. The 2023 mosaic of images only covered the southern portion of the reserve and there were no images available for 2024 (Figure A1). Coverage at Jobos Bay remained sufficient for annual maps throughout 2024 (Figure A2).
Map validation: Annual land-cover maps were validated using georeferenced field observations and expert visual interpretation of very-high-resolution base imagery available in Google Earth Pro. Field data were obtained from multi-year surveys at both reserves, including pre- and post-hurricane campaigns in 2014, 2018, and assessments of mangrove forest structure in 133 100 m2 plots at both reserves as part of the NERRS-MCC project in 2022-2023 (Ogurcak et al. in preparation). To avoid mixing pre‑ and post‑disturbance conditions in accuracy assessment, validation points within hurricane-affected areas were excluded because land cover at those locations changed after storm impact. Where field coverage was incomplete, additional validation points were assigned via visual interpretation by analysts familiar with local conditions to improve spatial representation. 
For each annual map, the classified land-cover value at each validation point was extracted and compared with the corresponding reference class. Land-cover classes in both the maps and validation dataset were aggregated into mangrove and non-mangrove categories to focus accuracy assessment on mangrove detection. We calculated Prevalence, Accuracy, Balanced Accuracy, Matthews Correlation Coefficient, Recall/Sensitivity, Precision, F1-score, Critical Success Index, and Specificity. General definitions provided in Table A3 (see [33] for standard performance metric formulations). Validation data were used solely for accuracy assessment and not in classifier training or map generation.
Mangrove change trajectory analysis: Mangrove change following Hurricanes Irma and Maria was examined using pixel-wise land-cover succession analysis for Rookery Bay (2018-2023) and Jobos Bay (2018-2024). To simplify interpretation, original classes were consolidated into broader categories (e.g., grouping several low-vegetation classes into mixed low-relief vegetation and merging sand into soil), and developed surfaces were excluded (Table A2). Trajectory analyses were constrained to a “mangrove target area” comprising pixels classified as mangrove in 2017 that retained valid classifications across all subsequent annual mosaics (Figure 2). Within this domain, we quantified annual class composition by calculating the proportion of pixels in each land-cover class, providing time series of class abundance for mangrove, unvegetated, and other categories (Figures 3-4; Tables 1-2). Pixel-wise transition trajectories were derived by tracking each pixel’s annual class assignment through time, yielding multi-year class sequences such as mangrove loss shown by pixel conversion to other classes in 2018, followed by recovery of mangrove or conversion to other classes during the study period. Dominant temporal pathways of disturbance and recovery were aggregated and expressed as percentages of the mangrove target area (Figures 5, S2-S5; Table 3), providing metrics of resistance, recovery, and prolonged degradation.
Transition to water pixels were observed in the 2018 images in both coastal and inland settings within the mangrove target areas (Figure 2). Water transitions along the coast indicate shoreline erosion, persistent inundation, or collapse of fringe mangroves. Transitions to water farther from the coast may reflect localized flooding, groundwater changes, altered drainage, or impoundment. To distinguish between these different processes, we generated a perennial water mask by adding water-class pixels across annual maps (2014-2023) and defining pixels classified as water in at least six of seven years as perennial open water. Small inland water bodies were manually removed from the perennial water mask. A permanent open-water mask was then obtained by applying morphological erosion using a disk-shaped structuring element (5-pixel radius) to reduce small-scale coastal variability. We calculated the distance to the permanent open-water mask boundary and applied a 20 m threshold (ten 2-m resolution pixels) to separate waters directly connected to the open coast from interior water bodies. Pixels within 20 m of the perennial water boundary were classified as “coastal water”, whereas those farther away were classified as “inland water”.

2.3. Satellite Time Series Analysis
To characterize disturbance, degradation, and recovery at finer temporal resolution, satellite surface-reflectance time series were constructed for polygons representing three mangrove condition classes: less-impacted, recovered, and non-recovered stands. These areas were identified by comparing NERRS-MCC land-cover maps from 2017 with 2018 and subsequent years and by visually interpreting interannual land-cover transitions (Figure 2).
Many non-recovered mangrove patches were approximately 100 m across or smaller, which would produce mixed pixels in coarser sensors. We therefore used Sentinel-2 imagery, which provides 10-20 m spatial resolution, 13 spectral bands from visible to shortwave-infrared, and a short revisit time [34,35]. Sentinel-2 Level-2A surface-reflectance data (COPERNICUS/S2_SR_HARMONIZED, [36]) were obtained from the data catalog of Google Earth Engine 
For each satellite pass, reflectance in each band was averaged within the polygons for each mangrove condition class. To reduce variability due to illumination and atmospheric changes while preserving relative spectral information (Figure S1), we normalized each band measurement by dividing reflectance in that band by the sum of reflectance across all bands before spatial averaging. Residual noise from cloud edges, thin clouds, and shadows was reduced using the Sentinel-2 Cloud Score+ mask [37], and only normalized, cloud-filtered reflectance values were retained (Figure S1c). From the cleaned time series, we computed the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) for each condition class and study area to track mangrove disturbance and recovery, using Sentinel-2 bands B8 (near-infrared) and B4 (red) as NDVI = (NIR − Red) / (NIR + Red) [38]. 
Because Sentinel-2 coverage at Jobos Bay begins in late 2018 and did not capture the immediate post-hurricane period after September 2017, Landsat 8 and 9 imagery (30 m resolution) were used to extend the time series. Landsat revisit time ranged from 16 days (Landsat 8 only) to 8 days after Landsat 9 launch, and the Operational Land Imager acquires visible to shortwave-infrared bands [39]. (USGS 2025). Landsat Level-2 Collection 2 Tier 1 data (LANDSAT/LC08/C02/T1_L2 and LANDSAT/LC09/C02/T1_L2, [40,41]) USGS 2026a, 2026b) were processed using the same band-normalization procedure as Sentinel-2, and each band was averaged over the polygons for each mangrove condition class. The NDVI was calculated from Landsat area-averaged bands B5 (NIR) and B4 (red). 
3. Results
3.1. Classification accuracy of annual land-cover maps
Annual habitat maps showed consistently high and temporally stable classification performance for both reserves (Table A3). In Rookery Bay, mangrove prevalence in the validation data was relatively low (0.22-0.61; mean 0.27), yet overall and balanced accuracy both averaged 0.93, and the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) was 0.83, indicating strong classification skill. Mangrove detection was robust, with recall, precision, and F1 scores of 0.94, 0.82, and 0.87, respectively, and specificity of 0.93 for non‑mangrove areas. In Jobos Bay, where mangrove prevalence was higher (0.60-0.70; mean 0.67), performance was similarly strong and stable. Overall and balanced accuracy both averaged 0.92, MCC averaged 0.83, and mangrove detection showed high recall (0.92), precision (0.96), and F1 score (0.94). Specificity remained high (0.93), indicating reliable discrimination of non‑mangrove pixels despite mangrove dominance.
These accuracy levels are consistent with recent mangrove mapping studies reporting overall accuracies of 0.85-0.97 and F1 scores ≥0.8 [42–44]. Given the consistently high overall and balanced accuracies in both regions (0.92-0.93), together with stable MCC, precision, and recall values, classification uncertainty was low and temporally stable. These performance metrics support the use of the annual maps for analyzing interannual mangrove variability. 
Within the 2017 mangrove study areas (Figure 2), the occurrence of class 9 (upland vegetation) occurred in subsequent annual maps but remained rare, and given the short time frame and site conditions, these occurrences are most consistent with classification error rather than true conversion. In both study areas, class 9 represented less than 2% of the mapped area in every annual map, averaging 0.7 ± 0.6% in Rookery Bay (2018-2023) and 1.3 ± 0.4% in Jobos Bay (2018-2024). These consistently low percentages provide an additional indication that misclassification of mangrove as upland vegetation was minimal and support the high classification accuracy reported above.
3.2. Spatial mangrove disturbance and recovery
3.2.1. Land cover post-storm changes
Hurricanes Irma and Maria in September 2017 produced clear impacts in the 2018 habitat maps for both reserves (Figures A1-A2), with extensive areas of mangrove forest transitioning to non‑mangrove classes. In contrast, the 2023 maps show little evidence of change following Hurricanes Ian and Fiona in September 2022.
The 2017 habitat maps, based on pre‑storm imagery, were used as baselines because mangrove extent matched that observed in 2014-2016. The 2018 maps, derived from imagery acquired in the first four months of the year, show widespread shifts from mangrove to the natural non‑vegetated soil class, consistent with field observations of defoliation and structural damage (broken branches, snapped trunks, uprooted trees). Loss of green canopy exposed the ground layer in the satellite imagery, so affected mangrove stands were classified as the underlying substrate visible after the storm (Figure 2). Many pixels that were mangrove in 2017 shifted to soil in 2018; others changed to low vegetation, consistent with exposed understory; and fringe mangrove pixels converted to water, indicating canopy loss over water and/or shoreline retreat. In subsequent years, some affected areas gradually reverted to mangrove (Figures A1-A2), revealing spatially heterogeneous recovery, whereas other regions remained non‑mangrove in 2023-2024. We quantified those changes using pixel-wise trajectories. 
3.2.2. Mangrove Recovery Pathways
A pixel-wise land-cover succession analysis was used to characterize annual class transitions and mangrove recovery trajectories. Transition pathways were derived by tracking each pixel’s class sequence through time within the mangrove target areas defined for each region (Figure 2). To simplify the analysis, trajectories were evaluated using a reduced set of classes (Table A2): mangrove, upland vegetation, mixed low-relief vegetation, natural non-vegetated soil, and water.
A key constraint on interpreting recovery timing is the absence of annual maps for 2019 in both reserves. The 2017 maps represent pre-storm conditions, and the 2018 maps capture immediate post-storm disturbance, but the next annual maps are from 2020. As a result, some areas mapped as “recovery” by 2020 may have recovered during 2019.
The time-series length also differed between reserves. In Rookery Bay, reduced spatial coverage in 2023 limited the target area to the southern portion of the reserve, and 2024 coverage was insufficient for annual mapping. Therefore, recovery pathways in Rookery Bay were evaluated over five annual maps (2018-2020-2021-2022-2023). In Jobos Bay, more complete coverage allowed inclusion of 2024, yielding six annual maps (2018-2020-2021-2022-2023-2024). Because the Jobos Bay time series includes one additional year and thus a larger number of possible trajectories, pathway results are reported as percentages rather than counts of unique paths to allow direct comparison between the reserves.
For the whole area of the land-cover maps (blue polygons in Figure 1), mangrove extent in 2017 totaled 307.0 km² in Rookery Bay and 9.6 km² in Jobos Bay, derived from pixel counts using latitude-corrected pixel area. Because only pixels with complete temporal records were retained (Figure 2), the mangrove target areas represented 24.2% and 70.0% of the 2017 mangrove extent map, respectively, due to reduced spatial coverage in Rookery Bay NERR, restricted to its southeast portion, and more complete coverage in Jobos Bay NERR. The study also includes the mangrove areas surrounding the NERRs. 
Results are summarized in Figures 3-5 and Tables 1-3. Figures 3-4 show annual class composition integrated within each mangrove target area. Figure 5 (with detailed views in Figures S2-S5) illustrates alluvial diagrams of class transitions for pixels classified as mangrove in 2017. Tables 1-2 present percentages of pixels transitioning from their original 2017 or 2018 class to each mapped class in target years integrated by mangrove target area; the first sections focus on 2017 mangroves, and subsequent rows describe dynamics of classes that appeared in 2018. Table 3 lists the most common annual class pathways for affected mangroves at the pixel level. Most trajectories involved transitions among mangrove, low vegetation, soil, and water classes, whereas only a small fraction resulted in persistent transitions away from mangrove. 
Compared to 2017, mangrove area declined sharply to 46% in Rookery Bay and 59% in Jobos Bay in 2018 in the mangrove target areas. By 2023, mangrove coverage had recovered to 90% in Rookery Bay and 83% in Jobos Bay by 2024 (Tables 1 and 2, Figures 2-4 and A1-A2). In contrast, no significant changes in mapped mangrove extent were observed after Hurricane Ian in Rookery Bay or Hurricane Fiona in Jobos Bay in 2022 (Figures A1-A2). 
For both reserves, areas that transitioned to short vegetation in 2018 showed the highest subsequent mangrove recovery, whereas areas that transitioned to water showed the lowest recovery. When mangrove recovery was expressed as the percentage of the area occupied by pixels that had changed class in the 2018 maps, Rookery Bay showed the highest recovery by 2023 for pixels that transitioned to short vegetation (91.9%), followed by those that transitioned to soil (84.9%), while pixels that transitioned to water showed the lowest recovery (51.5%). A similar pattern was observed in Jobos Bay by 2024, although recovery percentages were lower: 85.5% for short vegetation, 67.9% for soil, and 39.4% for water. 
3.2.2.1. Mangrove Recovery Pathways at Rookery Bay
2018 Mangrove class: Nearly half of the 2017 mangrove pixels remained classified as mangrove in 2018 (46%; Figure 3, Table 1). Most of these areas (83%) persisted as mangrove through 2020-2023 (Table 3). A small subset of this group (<7%) showed brief (1 year) transitions to short vegetation in subsequent years before returning to mangrove, a pattern consistent with temporary visibility of understory vegetation. 
2018 Soil class: Over 45% of 2017 mangrove pixels were reclassified as bare soil in 2018 (Table 1), especially along the eastern eyewall path of Hurricane Irma (Figures 1, A1). These areas exhibited a wide range of disturbance and recovery trajectories (Table 3, Figure 5, Figure S2). By 2023, 80.8% of soil pixels had returned to mangrove via two main mechanisms. The first, and most common recovery pathway, was a direct transition from soil to mangrove (69.2% of pixels across six paths), with most recovery occurring rapidly by 2020 (54.5%) and smaller fractions recovering in 2021 (5.6%) and 2022 (4.9%). The second mechanism involved a transition through short vegetation before mangrove recovery (11.6% of pixels across seven paths), typically with a one-year intermediate state. 
A small fraction of soil pixels followed more complex trajectories involving short vegetation or mangrove but did not end as mangrove by 2023 (3.1%; five paths). Nearly 6% of soil pixels never transitioned to any vegetation class, remaining soil or oscillating between soil and water across the five post-storm annual maps. 
2018 Low Vegetation class: A smaller portion of mangrove pixels (<7%) transitioned to mixed low-relief vegetation in 2018 (Table 1), consistent with exposed understory or short vegetation visible after canopy loss. By 2023, almost 92% of these pixels had returned to mangrove (Table 1, Figure S2), primarily via 3 recovery paths (Table 3). The dominant pathway was ‘rapid recovery to mangrove’ by 2020 (sequence 13-10-10-10-10; 74%), consistent with moderate canopy damage and fast regrowth. Other paths (~12.5%) involved persistence or temporary return to other vegetation before mangrove recovery in 2023, Only 0.1% of 2018 low-vegetation pixels converted to soil or water in 2020 and failed to return to any vegetation class in 2023.

The two more important paths (Table 3) were first, a rapid transition back to mangrove (24%) consistent with a fast canopy recovery, and second, the persistence of water pixels through the time series (10%). Overall, 48.0% of water pixels (33 paths) recovered to mangrove by 2023, mostly in 2020 (32.1%) and 2021 (10.9%), often with alternating classifications between water and soil. 
Transitions from water through short vegetation before mangrove recovery were rare (2.9%; five paths). A substantial 27.3% of water pixels (15 paths) did not return to any vegetation, remaining as water or soil, and 14.0% (35 paths) showed brief transitions to short vegetation or mangrove but did not end as mangrove by 2023. 
For pixels classified as water in 2018, we further distinguished coastal water from inland water to evaluate whether recovery patterns differed between shoreline-adjacent and interior sites. Mangrove pixels that transitioned to inland water tended to recover to mangrove more frequently than those transitioning to coastal water (61% vs. 48%; Table 1, Figure S3). Across the 2018 water class (66.5 ha), 51.5% of pixels recovered to mangrove by 2023, with 38.6% recovering early (2020) and 44.0% not returning to any vegetation. Coastal water pixels (49.5 ha) showed 48.3% recovery by 2023, 34.0% early recovery in 2020, and 47.0% with no vegetation recovery, whereas inland water pixels (17.0 ha) had higher recovery (60.9% by 2023, 52.2% by 2020) and lower recovery failure (35.4% non-vegetated). These patterns show lower mangrove recovery and a higher proportion of persistent non-vegetated pixels in coastal areas (Figure S6). 

3.2.2.2. Mangrove Recovery Pathways at Jobos Bay
Jobos Bay results are presented in direct comparison with Rookery Bay, focusing on percentages rather than counts of unique paths because the Jobos Bay time series includes one additional year, increasing the number of possible paths.
2018 mangrove class: In Jobos Bay, 58.6% of 2017 mangrove pixels remained classified as mangrove in 2018 (Figure 4, Table 2). Most of these areas (75.4%) persisted as mangrove through 2024 (Table 3). Approximately 13.6% of pixels showed temporary intrusions from other classes, and only 1.15% followed trajectories that did not return to mangrove by 2024, typically transitioning to soil with no subsequent return to any vegetated classes.
Mangrove coverage decreased in 2022 (Figure 4), resulting in the second-lowest mangrove area in the record after 2017. The areas no longer classified as mangrove were primarily reclassified as soil and low vegetation (Figure 4, Figure S4). Because the 2022 mosaic was generated using imagery acquired during the first four months of the year (Table A1), this decrease predates the September 2022 storm and therefore cannot be attributed to that event. 
2018 Soil class: Most mangrove pixels that became soil in 2018 returned to mangrove by 2024 (67.9%; Table 2, Figure 5), with nearly half recovering by 2020 (49.0%). However, a substantial fraction did not recover and remained as soil in 2024 (24.0%). As in Rookery Bay, the principal recovery mechanism was a direct transition from soil to mangrove (51.1%; Table 3, Figure S4), with most recovery occurring by 2020 (30.8%). Recovery via short vegetation was less frequent at Jobos Bay than at Rookery Bay (6.8%), with intermediate low-vegetation states typically lasting one year. Unlike Rookery Bay, a notable proportion of 2018 soil pixels at Jobos Bay did not return to mangrove or other vegetation (29.2%; Table 2, Figure S4). Many of these pixels (8.6%) remained soil throughout 2020-2024 (Table 3), while others oscillated between soil and water (4.4%) or had brief transitions to mangrove or low vegetation (3.9%). 
2018 Low Vegetation class: Similar to Rookery Bay, a small fraction of mangrove pixels (7.4%; Table 2, Figure 4) transitioned to mixed low-relief vegetation in 2018, consistent with exposed understory vegetation. Of these, 74.1% reverted to mangrove by 2020, increasing to 85.5% by 2024 (Table 2, Figure S4), which is lower than at Rookery Bay (89% by 2020 and 91.9% by 2023; Table 1). This is consistent with a notable fraction of low-vegetation pixels remaining as low vegetation in 2020 (9.6%) or transitioning to soil (13.2% in 2020; 7.8% in 2024). 
By 2024, 85.5% of 2018 low-vegetation pixels had returned to mangrove, mostly via 11 recovery paths. The most common trajectories involved fast recovery to mangrove by 2020 (two paths, 62.0%; Table 3), with an additional 7% recovering by 2021. However, 14.5% did not return to mangrove by 2024 (Figure S4), including a dominant path in which low vegetation converted to soil (2.2% of pixels). A small proportion ended in other vegetation classes (5.8%), predominantly short vegetation.

The overall 2018 water class (32.4 ha) showed ~40% recovery to mangrove by 2024, with 24% recovering by 2020. A large fraction of water pixels did not transition to vegetation by 2024 (58.9% remained soil or water), with non-vegetated pixels more frequent in coastal areas (64.2%) than inland (55.3%). A second area of concern is a small sector in the southwestern reserve where illegal expansion into protected lands has occurred. 3.3. Higher-temporal-resolution analysis of disturbance and recovery
Annual land-cover maps reveal storm-driven mangrove disturbance and recovery, but they cannot resolve shorter-term dynamics or seasonal variability. To capture these finer changes, high-temporal-resolution NDVI time series were analyzed for mangrove areas representing three post-storm response types: (a) less-impacted mangroves, (b) impacted but recovered mangroves, and (c) non-recovered mangroves. NDVI was derived from single-date Sentinel-2 passes for both reserves and complemented with Landsat 8/9 imagery to extend the early record at Jobos Bay. NDVI climatologies were calculated from less-impacted regions, excluding data within one year after the storm, to avoid contamination by immediate disturbance. Similarly to the analysis of the land-cover maps, NDVI detected the effect of the 2017 storms but not the 2022 storms.
3.3.1. Rookery Bay NDVI
Mangrove forests in Rookery Bay show clear NDVI seasonality (Figure 6), with maxima of 0.88-0.89 between November and February and a minimum of 0.82 in June. The high NDVI values and small seasonal amplitude (~0.07) are typical of evergreen mangrove canopy conditions. 
Less-impacted mangroves polygons still exhibited a post-storm NDVI decline, reaching 0.52 in October 2017 (about 0.16 below climatology), and recovering to climatological levels by September 2018 (Figure 6). These declines show that ‘less-impacted’ areas experienced measurable post-storm NDVI reductions and were not undisturbed. 
In regions that were impacted but eventually recovered (Figure 7), NDVI fell sharply after the storm and continued declining, reaching a minimum plateau between February and May of 2018 (0.49 ± 0.02; about 0.37 below climatology). Recovery occurred in two phases: a rapid increase in the second half of 2018, followed by a slower rise until climatological values were reached by mid-2022, although the seasonal NDVI cycle re-emerged earlier in 2019 (Figure 7). Non-recovered regions experienced a drastic NDVI decline immediately after the storm, reaching a minimum of 0.27 in May 2018 (Figure 8). NDVI remained low through 2025 (mean 0.34, SD 0.08), with a high temporal variability suggesting heterogeneous and changing surface conditions. Pre-storm NDVI in these areas was already lower and more variable than the mangrove climatology (0.7-0.8). Pumpkin Bay (Figure 8b) illustrates a location where lower pre-storm NDVI coincided with lack of post-storm recovery. A map of mangrove die-off in Rookery Bay NERR produced from a 1999 digital orthophoto quadrangle imagery (Jill Schmid, personal communication) documented mangrove die-off in the southern part of the lagoon in 1999.

3.3.2. Jobos Bay NDVI
In Jobos Bay, three regions capturing different impact and recovery levels were identified: (1) a less-impacted region in northeast Mar Negro, (2) an impacted but recovered region in northwestern Aguirre State Forest, and (3) a low-recovery region in eastern Aguirre State Forest, spatially separated from the recovered area (Figure 9).
Because there were no Sentinel-2 observations before 2019, NDVI from Landsat 8 and 9 was used to extend the record prior to Sentinel-2. Landsat and Sentinel-2 NDVI time series were plotted separately but interpreted jointly to track vegetation dynamics following Hurricane Maria. Inter-sensor consistency was assessed using polygon-averaged monthly NDVI pairs during the overlap period (2019-2025). Across polygons and months, mean biases ranged from -0.01 to -0.05 NDVI, mean absolute errors from 0.03 to 0.08, and root-mean-square errors from 0.045 to 0.087. These differences are small relative to the seasonal amplitude (~0.12) and much smaller than hurricane-related disturbances, as impacted regions showed month-to-month NDVI drops >0.30 after Maria (Figure 10). Inter-sensor discrepancies were minor compared with seasonal variability and disturbance magnitude, supporting combined interpretation of both data sets.
NDVI seasonality for Jobos Bay was defined from Sentinel-2 observations in the less-impacted polygon (2019-2025). Maximum NDVI and lowest variability occurred in December-January (mean 0.90 ± 0.01), whereas minimum NDVI and highest variability occurred in June-August (0.79 ± 0.04) (Figure 10).
Following Hurricane Maria (September 2017), the less-impacted region showed a NDVI decline to 0.68 in October (-0.18 relative to climatology), then a rapid rebound to 0.80 in November (+0.12 in one month). NDVI returned near climatological values by May 2018 (Figure 10, top). 
The impacted but recovered region experienced a larger NDVI reduction, reaching 0.52 in October 2017 (-0.34 from climatology). NDVI rose quickly in November (0.65; +0.13) and started to show seasonality in 2019 although with lower seasonal minimums. It took until 2022 to reach climatological conditions during all seasons, when seasonal low and high gradually matched pre-disturbance values, with both mean NDVI and seasonal amplitude restored (Figure 10, middle). NDVI recovery in Jobos Bay started over a month after the storm (November 2017), while in Rookery Bay NDVI continued to diminish several months after the storm, and recovery started in June 2018.
The low-recovery region in eastern Aguirre State Forest showed the strongest disturbance signal, with NDVI dropping to 0.42 in October 2017 (-0.44 relative to climatology; Figure 10, bottom). The subsequent rebound was limited (0.48 in November; +0.06), and NDVI did not return to pre-storm levels. Mean NDVI declined from a pre-storm average of 0.77 (SD 0.069) to a post-storm average of 0.56 (SD 0.07) and did not return to pre-storm levels.  Seasonality gradually re-emerged from late 2018 and became broadly synchronized with the regional climatological cycle by ~2023, but absolute NDVI remained markedly lower. By 2024, about half of this polygon showed mangrove recovery (Figure 9), so polygon-averaged NDVI reflects a mixture of mangrove vegetation, exposed soil, and open water, explaining the persistence of seasonal variability despite reduced mean NDVI. 
Together, the three polygons capture a gradient of recovery in Bahía de Jobos (Figures 9-10): (a) less-impacted, with recovery within months; (b) recovered with large initial decline followed by gradual recovery over ~3-4 years; and (c) low-recovery, with the strongest decline and incomplete recovery even eight years after the storm.

4. Discussion
4.1. High classification accuracy as a foundation for ecological attribution
Our time series of annual land-cover maps provided a robust basis for quantifying mangrove disturbance and recovery following major hurricanes in two contrasting reserves. Classification accuracies matched or exceeded those reported in recent regional and global mangrove mapping studies[9,42–44,46,47]. The consistently high overall and balanced accuracies (0.92-0.93), Matthews correlation coefficient (0.83), and complementary validation metrics across both reserves support interpreting interannual land-cover transitions as ecological change rather than classification error.
The use of high-resolution imagery, decision-tree classification with regionally tuned thresholds, and iterative stakeholder feedback further reduced confusion between mangroves and spectrally similar upland vegetation. These methodological choices enabled robust identification of pixel-level disturbance and recovery trajectories, differentiation between inland and coastal water responses, and detection of spatially coherent areas of persistent canopy loss. This approach extends earlier work that applied the same methodology to mangrove mapping, assessing Hurricane Irma impacts in southwest Florida, and benchmarking the classifier against alternative machine-learning approaches [7,10,15,16].  
A critical indicator of reliability was the low recurrence of spectrally similar upland vegetation (class 9) within the 2017 mangrove footprint (Figure 2) which remained below 2% in every annual map. This is consistent with the independently validated recall, precision, and F1 scores reported above, and together the two lines of evidence indicate that the combination of a decision-tree classifier and map post-processing effectively distinguished mangroves from terrestrial vegetation, including under conditions of canopy thinning or defoliation. The credibility of the mapped disturbance signal is further supported by the spatially and temporally coherent transition of 25-45% of the 2017 mangrove area to exposed soil in 2018, consistent with hurricane-driven disturbance rather than random classification variability.
This precision allowed us to trust the pixel-wise trajectory analysis; for example, a time sequence from soil to low vegetation to mangrove provided evidence of storm damage, progressive post-storm vegetation recovery, and re-establishment of mangrove cover, rather than an artifact of varying image quality or atmospheric conditions.
4.2. Wind exposure and threshold-dependent disturbance
The contrasting responses to the 2017 and 2022 hurricanes indicate that widespread, remotely detectable mangrove disturbance at both reserves was primarily associated with local exposure to hurricane-force winds rather than with storm rainfall or surge alone. Hurricanes Irma and Maria produced extensive canopy loss, marked NDVI declines, and transitions from mangrove to soil or water classes, whereas Hurricanes Ian and Fiona produced no comparable signal in either the annual land-cover maps or the dense NDVI time series, and 2023 field surveys indicated minimal defoliation from winds. This contrast is notable because Ian and Fiona were major hurricanes and generated substantial hydrologic forcing at the study sites, including an approximately 7-ft storm surge in Rookery Bay during Ian [24] and 20-25 in of rainfall in Jobos Bay during Fiona [25]. Nevertheless, the reserves were exposed mainly to strong tropical-storm-force winds in 2022 (≥ 50 kt), rather than the hurricane-force winds experienced in 2017 (≥ 64 kt) [22–25]. 
Storm track relative to each reserve helps explain this difference. During Irma, the eyewall crossed central Rookery Bay NERR, placing the southern portion of Rookery Bay analyzed here on the right side of the storm track, where the combination of forward storm motion and cyclonic circulation generally produces stronger winds, greater surge, and more severe wind damage [48]. In contrast, Ian made landfall approximately 100 km north of Rookery Bay. Although Ian produced a substantial surge in the region, local winds over the reserve were considerably weaker than those associated with Irma. Similarly, Jobos Bay was approximately 40 km from Maria’s track in 2017 and experienced hurricane-force winds, whereas Fiona made landfall approximately 100 km west of the reserve in 2022, exposing the site to extreme rainfall but weaker winds. Thus, storm category at landfall alone did not determine local ecological disturbance. Instead, the local wind field and a reserve’s position relative to the storm track determined whether wind exposure was sufficient to generate widespread canopy damage.
Our results therefore suggest an apparent threshold-dependent response, in which widespread spectral evidence of disturbance emerged when local wind speeds reached hurricane force, but not when exposure remained below that level. This should not be interpreted as a universal 64-kt ecological threshold, because wind damage depends on storm duration and gust characteristics, species composition, stand height and canopy structure, antecedent rainfall and soil conditions, and local topographic and hydrogeomorphic setting [4,49]. Rather, hurricane-force wind exposure appears to be the primary threshold associated with extensive defoliation and structural damage in these two systems. This interpretation is consistent with Imbert [49] results of maximum mangrove basal area loss at wind speeds ≥ 130 km h-1 (70 kt), and with Taillie et al. [50], who found maximum wind speed to be the strongest predictor of hurricane-related mangrove damage, with stronger effects than surge and rainfall, although flooding, forest structure, and prior disturbance also contributed to observed impacts. 
The different signals observed by the two remote-sensing analyses are important for interpreting this result. The annual land-cover maps captured major structural disturbance, including canopy removal sufficient to expose the under-canopy class. The NDVI time series captured changes in green canopy condition, including defoliation and reduced photosynthetic activity that may occur without a persistent land-cover transition. Neither approach directly measures hydrologic stress, salinity change, soil redox conditions, root damage, or sublethal physiological stress. Consequently, the absence of a detectable 2022 signal does not demonstrate that Ian and Fiona had no ecological effects. Instead, it indicates that their local wind, rainfall, and surge exposure did not produce widespread or persistent canopy damage detectable as NDVI decline or land-cover conversion at the spatial and temporal resolution of this study.
This distinction helps explain why extreme rainfall and surge in 2022 did not produce a spectral response comparable to 2017. Storm surge, sediment transport, and severe rainfall can alter porewater salinity, hydroperiod, soil nutrients, soil oxygen availability, and redox conditions, but the effects on forest canopy may be delayed, spatially heterogeneous, or buffered where elevation, drainage, tidal exchange, and sediment accretion remain adequate [51,52]. In contrast, hurricane-force winds can cause immediate defoliation, branch breakage, stem failure, uprooting, and canopy collapse [2,8], producing abrupt changes in visible and near-infrared reflectance that are readily detected by NDVI and high-resolution land-cover classification [53]. The 2022 storms therefore provided an unintentional natural experiment: even with extreme rainfall at Jobos Bay and substantial surge at Rookery Bay, the absence of a concurrent widespread canopy signal indicates that wind exposure was the dominant proximal driver of the detectable disturbance observed after the 2017 hurricanes.
4.3. Recovery was high but spatially heterogeneous
Despite the severity of the 2017 storm disturbances, most affected mangrove areas eventually recovered; however, recovery was strongly heterogeneous in space and time. At both reserves, a substantial fraction of the 2017 mangrove area remained classified as mangrove throughout the study period, indicating high resistance or rapid recovery in many stands. Among pixels that transitioned to non-mangrove classes in 2018, the majority returned to mangrove within 2-3 years, especially where the initial post-storm classes were bare soil or low vegetation. In Rookery Bay, mangrove coverage declined to 46% in 2018, recovered to 81% in 2020, and reached 90% in 2023. In Jobos Bay, mangrove coverage declined to 59% in 2018, recovered to 73% in 2020, and reached 83% in 2024. Similar recovery times have been observed in Florida Everglades mangroves, where aboveground carbon stocks lost during hurricanes were largely recovered within approximately four years [6]. A smaller but non-trivial proportion of spatially clustered pixels exhibited persistent degradation and limited recovery, particularly along coastal fringes and in both hydrologically altered inland locations or within lower elevation basin forests. These patterns underscore that while mangrove forests in these two regions are highly resilient to strong storms, recovery is not uniform or guaranteed, and local environmental conditions strongly modulate post-disturbance trajectories. Mangrove responses to hurricanes span a continuum from high resistance to delayed recovery and, in some locations, transition toward persistent, degraded states.
Our trajectory analysis revealed two dominant recovery mechanisms following severe canopy loss: direct transitions from bare soil to mangrove, and recovery from mixed low-relief vegetation to mangrove. The first mechanism, which accounted for most of the recovering area in both reserves, consisted of pixels remaining classified as bare soil for one or more years before transitioning directly to mangrove. The one-year transitions, the most common pathway, suggest rapid canopy recovery, consistent with damage that was primarily limited to canopy defoliation and minor branch loss rather than severe branch loss and widespread tree mortality. Longer transitions from bare soil to mangrove may reflect more severe canopy or structural damage, with recovery occurring through resprouting from surviving trees, including basal or epicormic shoots in the species Laguncularia racemosa and Avicennia germinans. Resprouting individuals of both species were commonly observed in the mangrove structural assessment conducted in both reserves (2022 - 2023). In Rookery Bay, transition of 2018 soil pixels through short vegetation before mangrove recovery (11.6%), possibly points to recruitment and establishment of mangrove propagules.
The second mechanism involved one or two years of mixed low-relief vegetation before transitioning to mangrove. Following canopy loss, the exposed surface likely included a mixture of understory vegetation including mangrove saplings and herbaceous succulents such as Batis maritima and Sesuvium portulacastrum, which were observed at the mangrove plots sampled post-hurricane. The relatively short duration of this intermediate stage (typically one to two years) suggests that these areas experienced either a combination of less severe impact and rapid recovery or recovery through resprouting and/or advance regeneration (i.e., seedlings and saplings that can take advantage of increased light levels following storm-induced canopy thinning) [5]. Overall, the predominance of short recovery pathways indicates that post-hurricane mangrove recovery was driven primarily by the recovery of surviving trees rather than by prolonged, multi-stage successional replacement.
Areas that transitioned from mangrove in 2017 to water in 2018 exhibited substantially lower recovery rates related to their area (51.5% for Rookery Bay in 2023, and 39.4% for Jobos Bay in 2024) than those transitioning to soil or low vegetation. Lower mangrove recovery was particularly evident along exposed coastal fringes, where persistent water or soil states were more prevalent, indicating geomorphic and hydrologic changes such as shoreline erosion, prolonged inundation, storm-surge ponding, or loss of substrate elevation that can inhibit recolonization over ecologically relevant time scales. In Rookery Bay, red mangroves (Rhizophora mangle) dominate the shoreline and fringe zones and may be especially vulnerable to severe structural damage because recovery is limited to advance regeneration and propagule recruitment, as this species does not exhibit epicormic growth. Similar patterns in recovery failure were documented across southwest Florida (including the current study area) following Hurricane Irma using a combination of Landsat imagery and lidar derived DEM, highlighting the vulnerability of low elevation mangrove areas to ponding and prolonged inundation [8]. Similarly, lower mangrove recovery has been found in fringe mangrove, especially for Rhizophora mangle in northeast Caribbean [49]. In contrast, inland water pixels more frequently transitioned to soil or back to mangrove, suggesting that inland areas may experience sediment deposition or hydrologic adjustments that eventually allow vegetation re-establishment. Smith et al. [4] reported that some Everglades mangrove forests recovered after catastrophic hurricane disturbance, whereas others were permanently converted to mudflats, with damage severity varying among geomorphic settings. Coastal fringe zones in our study reserves appeared more vulnerable to persistent erosion, hydrologic alteration, and structural collapse of mangrove stands.
These contrasting trajectories also have important implications for blue-carbon storage and coastal protection. Areas that recovered structurally and functionally are likely to regain at least part of their carbon sequestration capacity and shoreline stabilization functions over decadal time scales [1,6]. In contrast, areas that transitioned to persistent water or non-vegetated soil may represent long-term reductions in carbon storage, diminished resistance to future storm surges, and possible shifts toward alternative ecosystem states such as mudflats or open water. Identifying these persistent degradation hotspots is therefore important for prioritizing hydrologic restoration, erosion mitigation, and long-term ecological monitoring within mangrove reserves.
Both regions showed examples of limited mangrove recovery associated with pre-existing stressors. In eastern Aguirre State Forest and along Camino del Indio, persistent canopy loss occurred in areas affected by impounded hydrology and development encroachment, respectively, suggesting that pre-existing environmental degradation reduced post-storm resilience. In Rookery Bay, an inland lagoon around Pumpkin Bay had documented mangrove die-offs in 1999 (Jill Schmid, personal communication). Although mangrove cover was present in 2017, this area showed little to no mangrove recovery following Hurricane Irma. Previous studies have likewise shown that mangrove forests affected by hydrologic alteration or other forms of degradation are less likely to recover naturally following major storm impacts [2,54]. Lewis et al. [55] describe the chronic stressors and mechanisms that can lead to mass mortality following acute disturbances such as hurricanes as mangrove heart-attacks.
4.4. Differences in recovery between the two sites
Although both reserves exhibited substantial recovery following the 2017 hurricanes, recovery was less complete in Jobos Bay than in Rookery Bay over the same period. Mangrove cover had recovered to 83% of its 2017 extent in Jobos Bay by 2024, compared with 90% in Rookery Bay by 2023. Jobos Bay also showed lower recovery of pixels that transitioned to soil or low vegetation in 2018, as well as a higher proportion of persistent non-vegetated trajectories. These differences may partly reflect the greater extent of pre-existing natural and anthropogenic stressors in Jobos Bay, in addition to differences in hydrology, geomorphic setting, local disturbance exposure, and climatic conditions. 
Persistent degradation in Jobos Bay was particularly evident in eastern Aguirre State Forest and along Camino del Indio. Eastern Aguirre State Forest contained a large area with only partial mangrove recovery, consistent with the combined influence of pre-existing environmental stressors and post-hurricane disturbance (Figure S7). Along Camino del Indio, illegal expansion into protected lands appears to have compounded storm-related canopy loss, with storm-damaged mangrove areas subsequently cleared or altered (Figure S8; Angel Dieppa, personal communication). These examples indicate that hurricane disturbance can interact with both chronic stress and coastal governance issues, reducing the likelihood of natural recovery and increasing the persistence of non-mangrove states.
Jobos Bay showed an anomalous reduction in mangrove coverage in the 2022 map (Figure 4). Because the 2022 mosaic was generated from imagery acquired during the first four months of the year (Table A1), this reduction cannot be attributed to Hurricane Fiona (September 2022). During plot sampling in 2022, numerous recently dead or stressed small stems (both small trees and sprouts associated with epicormic growth) were recorded in areas of mangroves that had evidence of post-hurricane recovery; mortality appeared to be associated with very dry field conditions observed at the affected sites (Ogurcak et al., in preparation). These observations are consistent with the exceptionally low precipitation recorded in 2021 (441 mm), at Jobos Bay NERR, which was the lowest annual precipitation in the 2001-2024 record (long-term annual mean of 955 ± 319 mm; station JOBJBMET; [56]).
The overall patterns observed here are consistent with studies showing that post-cyclone mangrove recovery depends not only on storm intensity but also on antecedent ecosystem condition, local hydrology, and geomorphic exposure. Asbridge et al. [57] reported broad post-cyclone recovery in Australian mangroves but identified localized areas of persistent damage, while Velázquez-Salazar et al. [3] found highly variable recovery after Hurricane Roslyn in Mexico, with limited recovery in previously degraded forests. These findings support the interpretation that lower recovery in Jobos Bay was likely influenced by the interaction between hurricane damage and pre-existing stressors rather than by storm exposure alone. Recovery may have been further constrained by weather extremes, including the exceptionally dry conditions recorded in 2021. 
Another important difference was that the 2017 post-storm NDVI in Rookery Bay exhibited a deeper and more prolonged functional decrease than in Jobos Bay. In polygons that were damaged in 2018 but subsequently recovered, NDVI began increasing in Jobos Bay shortly after Hurricane Maria, by November 2017. In Rookery Bay, NDVI continued to decline after Hurricane Irma and did not begin to recover until June 2018. A similar NDVI decline after the storm is shown in Lagomasino et al. [8] for southern Florida after hurricane Irma. By mid-2022, however, both reserves had returned to their climatological mean NDVI values and seasonal amplitudes.
The delayed NDVI recovery in southern Rookery Bay is consistent with the greater initial disturbance associated with its position immediately to the right of Hurricane Irma’s track. The large extent of Rookery Bay NERR provides a clear example of how hurricane impacts can vary according to position relative to the storm track. Hurricane Irma's eye crossed approximately the central portion of the reserve (Figure 1), placing the section analyzed in this study (southern Rookery Bay) on the right side of the storm track, where the strongest winds and storm surge occurred. This area experienced extensive canopy loss and a prolonged decline in NDVI following the storm. In contrast, areas left of the storm track (north section of Rookery Bay, not analyzed in this study) were less affected and retained a greater proportion of mangrove cover in the 2018 map [17]. This spatial asymmetry is consistent with previous observations that mangrove damage in Florida is typically greater on the right side of landfalling hurricanes than on the left side at comparable distances from the storm center [48]. By contrast, Jobos Bay was located 40 km from the left side of Hurricane Maria track, where wind and surge exposure were lower than near the storm’s right-front quadrant. Greater wind-driven canopy and structural damage in southern Rookery Bay could have prolonged the decline in NDVI by delaying leaf replacement and canopy rebuilding. However, higher surge and rainfall exposure at Rookery Bay in 2017 may also have contributed to the prolonged post-storm NDVI decline. For Hurricane Irma, Castañeda-Moya et al. [58] found that storm deposits extended up to 10-km inland, with vertical accretion several times higher than the long-term values, and Radabaugh et al. [52] found delayed mortality after the storm due to mud storm surge deposit. Storm surge and rainfall may prolong stress through salinity changes, sustained inundation, low soil oxygen availability, or altered sediment and nutrient conditions. Such effects could suppress leaf production or increase delayed mortality after the storm, even where the initial loss of canopy was predominantly wind-driven. 
4.5. Structural and functional recovery
The annual land-cover trajectories and NDVI time series captured complementary dimensions of recovery. Land-cover transitions represented substantial structural change, including canopy loss sufficient to expose soil or water and subsequent return to a mangrove canopy class. In contrast, NDVI captured functional canopy conditions, including defoliation and reductions in photosynthetic activity that did not necessarily result in a mapped land-cover transition.
NDVI indicated a two-steps recovery process in areas disturbed by the 2017 hurricanes that eventually recovered: after the sharp decline immediately after the storms there was an initial rapid rebound during the first post-storm year, followed by slower recovery through mid-2022 when seasonality was evident. The early rebound phase likely reflected leaf replacement, resprouting, and partial canopy closure, whereas the later phase may represent continued biomass accumulation and structural recovery [3,6]. The presence of an NDVI decline in less severely affected areas where pixels remained classified as mangrove in 2018, shows that functional disturbance extended beyond the area of mapped structural conversion. Conversely, the lack of an NDVI decline in 2022 indicates that the weaker local winds associated with Ian and Fiona did not produce widespread, detectable canopy-level functional disturbance. Annual averages of vegetation indices have been used to monitor post-storm mangrove recovery [3,9,50,59]. However, the study of NDVI from single-date satellite overpasses, rather than from annual composites, allowed a detailed assessment of recovery timing in our study areas.
Combining very-high-resolution land-cover maps with dense NDVI time series allowed us to evaluate disturbance and recovery across complementary spatial and temporal scales. Land-cover trajectories of the annual maps identified pathways of structural recovery, whereas NDVI time series characterized changes in vegetation condition and recovery dynamics that preceded complete canopy restoration. By integrating these approaches, this framework provides managers with spatially explicit and temporally resolved information to support hydrologic restoration, shoreline stabilization, monitoring of persistent dieback, and prioritization of vulnerable sectors within protected estuaries. Because the workflow relies primarily on transferable remote-sensing datasets and reproducible classification approaches, it offers a scalable approach that can be adapted to other NERRs and mangrove regions exposed to repeated tropical cyclone disturbance, providing a template for combined structural-functional resilience assessment.
4.6. Methodological considerations and limitations
The combination of high-resolution land-cover maps, pixel-level trajectory analysis, stakeholder-informed corrections, and dense NDVI time series enabled a detailed assessment of post-storm mangrove recovery. Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered. First, differences in image availability introduced temporal and spatial asymmetries between reserves. Annual mosaics were generated using imagery from the first four months of each year when possible; however, this was not always feasible. As a result, some annual maps were derived from imagery acquired in different seasons, potentially introducing phenological and spectral inconsistencies between years. In addition, no land-cover maps were available for 2019, a key period for early recovery, and WorldViewTM coverage declined after 2020, particularly in Rookery Bay, where no imagery was available for 2024 and 2023 coverage was limited to the southern portion of the reserve. Second, despite high classification accuracies, residual errors and mixed pixels are unavoidable in heterogeneous coastal environments, especially near ecotones and heavily disturbed areas. Third, NDVI reflects vegetation greenness rather than species composition, forest structure, or belowground processes. Consequently, areas that appear spectrally recovered may still differ from pre-storm conditions in biomass or structural complexity, while persistently low NDVI may reflect mixtures of sparse vegetation, exposed sediment, and shallow water rather than complete ecosystem collapse. These limitations may influence interpretation at local scales but do not alter the broader pattern of widespread recovery alongside localized persistent degradation observed across both reserves.
5. Conclusions
Annual 2-m resolution land-cover maps provided a basis for quantifying hurricane-related mangrove disturbance and recovery. Decision-tree classifications achieved consistently high performance across both reserves (overall and balanced accuracies of 0.92-0.93; MCC = 0.83), supporting interpretation of observed land-cover trajectories as ecological change. The very low confusion with upland vegetation further supported detection of canopy loss and subsequent recovery in these complex coastal environments.
The 2017 hurricanes caused extensive but incomplete canopy disturbance, whereas the 2022 storms produced little detectable canopy response. One year after the 2017 storms, 46% of the 2017 mangrove area in Rookery Bay and 59% in Jobos Bay remained classified as mangrove, indicating substantial resistance in addition to subsequent recovery. In contrast, neither the annual land-cover maps nor the NDVI time series showed important disturbance following the 2022 storms. These contrasting responses suggest that direct local exposure to hurricane-force winds in 2017 was the primary proximal driver of the widespread canopy disturbance detectable in this study, whereas extreme rainfall and storm surge without comparable wind exposure in 2022 did not produce a similar spectral response. This apparent wind-related threshold should not be interpreted as a universal ecological threshold, because storm duration, gusts, forest structure, antecedent condition, hydrology, and geomorphology also influence mangrove damage. 
Mangrove recovery was substantial but spatially and temporally heterogeneous. Mangrove cover recovered to 90% of its 2017 extent by 2023 in Rookery Bay and to 83% by 2024 in Jobos Bay. Most disturbed pixels that transitioned to bare soil or low vegetation returned to mangrove within 2-3 years, indicating that recovery was dominated by regrowth of surviving trees, resprouting, and/or advance regeneration rather than prolonged successional replacement. In contrast, pixels that transitioned to water had substantially lower recovery rates, particularly along exposed coastal fringes, consistent with persistent inundation, erosion, ponding, or other geomorphic and hydrologic changes that can limit recolonization. Persistent degradation was also associated with pre-existing hydrologic and anthropogenic stressors, demonstrating that antecedent conditions can constrain post-hurricane resilience.
NDVI provided a complementary measure of functional canopy response. Disturbed areas showed an initial rapid rebound during the first post-storm year followed by slower recovery through approximately mid-2022, consistent with progressive canopy rebuilding. NDVI also detected reductions in vegetation condition in areas that remained classified as mangrove in 2018, demonstrating that functional disturbance extended beyond areas of mapped structural conversion. Recovery timing differed between reserves: NDVI recovery began in Jobos Bay by November 2017 but was delayed until June 2018 in southern Rookery Bay, consistent with the greater disturbance associated with its position on the stronger right side of Hurricane Irma. Although stronger surge and rainfall exposure may also have contributed to the prolonged response in Rookery Bay, their effects cannot be separated from wind exposure with the available data.
The integration of very-high-resolution land-cover trajectories with temporal dense NDVI time series provides a transferable framework for distinguishing structural and functional dimensions of mangrove resilience following tropical cyclones. The approach identifies both rapidly recovering forests and persistent degradation hotspots, providing spatially explicit information that can support long-term monitoring, hydrologic restoration, shoreline management, and conservation planning in mangrove ecosystems increasingly exposed to intense tropical storms.
