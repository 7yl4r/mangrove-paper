Highlights
What are the main findings?
High-spatial-resolution (~2 m) annual land-cover maps show that the 2017 hurricanes caused extensive loss of mapped mangrove canopy cover: within the areas analyzed, about half of the pre-storm mangrove class remained one year after the storms in both reserves, and most of it was mapped as mangrove again within six to seven years. Reclassification to mangrove was lowest where disturbed pixels had become coastal water.
Monthly Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) time series from Sentinel-2 and Landsat show a rapid rebound of canopy greenness within the first post-storm year, followed by a slower multi-year return to climatological values and seasonality in both reserves.
What are the implications of the main findings?
Extensive but largely reversible loss of mapped canopy cover indicates high resilience in both reserves, whereas the low rate at which coastal-fringe pixels and pixels affected by pre-existing stressors return to the mangrove class flags areas at risk of persistent degradation.
NDVI results indicate that canopy greenness is regained rapidly after hurricanes but takes several years to return to pre-storm levels and seasonal amplitude, which is useful for designing post-storm monitoring and for timing field assessments.
Abstract
Two U.S. National Estuarine Research Reserves (NERRs), Rookery Bay (Florida) and Jobos Bay (Puerto Rico), were near major hurricanes in 2017 (Irma, Maria) and 2022 (Ian, Fiona). We tracked changes in mapped mangrove canopy cover using pixel-wise annual land-cover trajectories from 2-m WorldView™ imagery (2017-2024), together with monthly Sentinel-2 and Landsat NDVI time series (2016-2025). Trajectories were computed within mangrove target areas of 4,298.5 ha at Rookery Bay and 620.4 ha at Jobos Bay — pixels mapped as mangrove in 2017 that retained valid classifications in every later mosaic, covering 14% and 65% of the 2017 mangrove extent mapped at each site. Because the Rookery Bay domain also lies in that reserve’s most damaged sector, all values describe the analyzed areas rather than whole reserves. One year after the 2017 storms, the mangrove class had fallen to 46% (Rookery Bay) and 59% (Jobos Bay) of the target areas, with most disturbed pixels mapped as bare soil. Mangrove cover was subsequently regained across most of both areas, reaching 90% and 79% in 2023 (83% at Jobos Bay by 2024). Recovery was ordered by post-storm surface state, highest for pixels that became low vegetation and lowest for those converted to coastal water, which recovered by under half at both sites; persistent degradation was spatially clustered and coincided with pre-existing hydrologic stressors or human development. NDVI fell sharply after the storms, rebounded within the first post-storm year, and returned gradually to climatological values and seasonality by about mid-2022, beginning earlier at Jobos Bay (November 2017) than in southern Rookery Bay (June 2018). No widespread canopy signal from the 2022 storms was detected at the resolution analyzed, a contrast consistent with local hurricane-force wind exposure being the dominant proximal control on detectable canopy disturbance, although four site-storm combinations cannot separate wind from rainfall, surge, and antecedent condition. Combining annual canopy-class maps with dense NDVI series offers a transferable framework for tracking mangrove disturbance and locating persistent post-storm degradation.
Keywords: National Estuarine Research Reserves; Jobos Bay, Rookery Bay; coastal resilience; mangrove extent; land-cover maps; land-cover trajectories; NDVI; remote sensing; coastal resilience.


1. Introduction
Mangroves provide critical ecosystem services by stabilizing shorelines, reducing storm-surge impacts, and mitigating coastal flooding for coastal communities. Loss or degradation of coastal mangroves can significantly increase the land area exposed to damaging surge inundation [1]. Tropical cyclones are major disturbances to mangrove forests: strong winds, storm surge, and coastal erosion can uproot trees, defoliate canopies, and alter species composition and stand structure [2,3]. Severe storms can also modify hydrology, salinity, sediment deposition, and soil redox conditions, including sulfide concentrations, with consequences ranging from seedling mortality to widespread death of mature stands [2,4]. The effects of tropical cyclones can be cumulative, with wind, surge, and rainfall jointly influencing post-disturbance recovery conditions [5]. Many mangrove forests show rapid functional recovery of canopy leaf area and carbon exchange after major disturbances, returning to pre-storm conditions within a few years [3,6], although structural recovery can be slower and some stands remain degraded.
Satellite remote sensing provides a powerful tool for monitoring the distribution and area extent of mangrove ecosystems across large spatial scales. Time series of annual and seasonal mangrove mosaics have been used to quantify hurricane-related canopy damage and track recovery following storms [3,7–9]. Monitoring mangrove forests is valuable to managers seeking to conserve habitat quality and sustain ecosystem services [10].
Hurricane impacts on mangroves and their subsequent recovery have been documented across a wide range of regions using satellite time series, and that literature frames the contribution of the present study. In the Atlantic and Caribbean basin, Taillie et al. [50] mapped damage from the 2017 hurricane season across the Gulf of Mexico and the Caribbean and found maximum wind speed to be the strongest predictor of mangrove damage; Lagomasino et al. [8] combined Landsat imagery with lidar-derived elevation to show that storm surge and ponding explained the extent of mangrove dieback in southwest Florida after Irma; and Radabaugh et al. [52] documented delayed mortality at two Irma landfall sites. Outside the United States, Imbert [49] related mangrove basal-area loss to wind speed in the East Caribbean, Asbridge et al. [57] used the Landsat archive to separate short- and long-term cyclone effects on Australian mangroves, and Velazquez-Salazar et al. [3] tracked early canopy recovery after Hurricane Roslyn in Mexico. Vegetation-index time series are the most widely used tool in this work: NDVI and related indices from Landsat, Sentinel-2, and MODIS have been used to detect defoliation, to date the onset of regreening, and to separate recovering from persistently degraded stands after cyclones in Mexico [3,53], the British Virgin Islands [59], and Australia [57]. Most of these analyses rely on 10-30 m imagery, on annual or seasonal composites, or on both, which limits the detection of small degraded patches and the resolution of recovery timing within a year. The present study differs in two respects: the structural component uses annual 2-m canopy-class maps, which resolve degraded patches of tens of metres, and the spectral component uses NDVI from individual satellite overpasses rather than composites, which resolves the timing of decline and regreening to within weeks. In the United States, two National Estuarine Research Reserves (NERRs), namely Rookery Bay in southwest Florida, and Jobos Bay in south-central Puerto Rico (Figure 1), have coastlines dominated by mangrove habitats that are frequently exposed to tropical cyclones. In September 2017, Hurricane Irma passed over Rookery Bay, and Hurricane Maria passed very close to Jobos Bay, affecting mangroves in both reserves. In September 2022, Hurricanes Ian and Fiona again passed near both reserves (Figure 1). Collaborative research projects were implemented to characterize storm impacts on mangroves: “Resilience of the Mangrove Coast” [11] and “Limits of Resilience” [12], hereafter referred to as the NERRS Mangrove Coast Collective (NERRS-MCC). These projects generated a diverse set of products to improve understanding of long-term mangrove disturbance and recovery under repeated tropical-cyclone exposure [13,14]. Among the primary outputs of the NERRS-MCC projects was an annual time series (2014-2024) of high-resolution (2-m) land-cover maps derived from WorldViewTM imagery using a decision-tree classification [15,16]. 
The aim of this study was to characterize and quantify post-storm changes in mangrove cover using two complementary approaches: satellite-derived land-cover maps and spectral time series. We focused on mangrove resistance, recovery, and identifying areas that showed persistent degradation following the 2017 storms. Post-storm classification maps (2018) revealed widespread transitions from mangroves to other land-cover classes at both reserves, as canopy loss and downed trees exposed the underlying substrate [17]. We quantified pixel-wise land-cover change from 2017 to 2024 at Jobos Bay and from 2017 to 2023 in the southeastern portion of Rookery Bay. We also examined the time series (2016-2025) of the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) derived from Sentinel-2 and Landsat imagery at spatial resolution of 10 and 30 m pixels to characterize storm disturbance and post-storm recovery timing at finer (monthly means and single-date observations) temporal resolution.

2. Materials and Methods
2.1. Study Areas and Hurricane Disturbance
The study focused on the Rookery Bay and Jobos Bay National Estuarine Research Reserves (NERRs) and adjacent coastal ecosystems. Rookery Bay is in southwest Florida and Jobos Bay is located south-central Puerto Rico. Mangrove ecosystems in south Florida and Puerto Rico are composed of three true mangrove tree species: Avicennia germinans (black mangrove), Laguncularia racemosa (white mangrove), and Rhizophora mangle (red mangrove) [19]. Within the boundaries of Jobos Bay and Rookery Bay NERRs, mangrove ecosystems cover approximately 1,000 and 31,000 acres, respectively [20]. Mangrove structure in both reserves ranges from scrub forests composed of small individuals (<2 m) to taller, forested stands with trees typically 10–15 m in height. Mixed-species assemblages are common in both reserves, along with tidally-dominated fringing forests characterized primarily by red mangrove and lower-elevation basin forests dominated by black mangrove [21]. 
We focused our temporal sampling to capture mangrove cover and extent before and after four hurricanes that affected the study regions: Irma (2017) and Ian (2022) affected southwest Florida, and Maria (2017) and Fiona (2022) that impacted Puerto Rico. Storm exposure was characterized using National Hurricane Center best track and wind swath data [18] to document trajectories and wind fields affecting each reserve (Figure 1). 
During the September 2017 storms, both reserves experienced hurricane-force winds (≥64 kt; Figure 1a,b). Hurricane Irma made landfall near Marco Island, directly affecting the center of Rookery Bay NERR as a Category 3 storm with maximum sustained winds of about 100 kt, storm tides of 6-10 ft above ground level (AGL), and 10-15 in of rainfall across the southern portion of Rookery Bay [22]. Hurricane Maria made landfall about 40 km east of Jobos Bay NERR as a Category 4 storm with maximum sustained winds of 135 kt, generating storm tides of around 4 ft AGL along Jobos Bay and 7-10 in of rainfall in the area [23]. 
During the September 2022 storms, both reserves were exposed to strong tropical-storm-force winds (≥50 kt; Figure 1c,d) but remained outside the core band of hurricane-force wind fields. Hurricane Ian made landfall as a Category 4 storm approximately 100 km north of Rookery Bay, producing maximum inundation of about 7 ft AGL near Marco Island and 5-7 in of rainfall in the surrounding area [24]. Hurricane Fiona made landfall as a Category 1 storm approximately 100 km west of Jobos Bay, generating 1-3 ft of storm surge inundation AGL along the southern coast of Puerto Rico and heavy rainfall of 20-25 in around Jobos Bay [25]. These contrasting exposure conditions allow comparison of mangrove responses to severe (2017) versus less intense (2022) tropical-cyclone impacts and assess the effects of repeated disturbances. 
2.2. Annual Land-Cover Mapping and Recovery Analysis
Provenance of the products used: The annual land-cover maps analyzed here were not produced for this manuscript. They are existing products of the NERRS-MCC projects, distributed with their own documentation, metadata, and accuracy assessment [13,14,32]. The subsections below headed “Satellite imagery and preprocessing”, “Annual land-cover maps”, and “Map validation” summarize that earlier work so that the reader can judge the inputs; the imagery selection, the classifier, the mosaicking rules, the post-processing, and the validation design and accuracy metrics reported in Table A3 are fixed properties of those products and were not modified here. What was carried out for the present manuscript is the analysis built on top of them: definition of the mangrove target areas, the pixel-wise trajectory and pathway analysis, the separation of coastal from inland water, the delineation of the mangrove condition polygons, and the NDVI time-series analysis of Section 2.3. One consequence of this division of labour is that the accuracy assessment available to us was designed to evaluate the annual maps, not the multi-year transitions that are the subject of this paper; the implications are set out in Sections 3.1 and 4.8.
NERRS-MCC produced a time series of 2-m annual land-cover maps spanning 2014-2024 for Rookery Bay and Jobos Bay NERRs and the surrounding ecosystems extending beyond the reserve boundaries (Figure 1, blue polygons). The maps used a decision-tree classifier developed by McCarthy et al. [15,16]. This hierarchical classifier assigns each pixel to a land-cover class through a sequence of binary decision rules and has been previously applied to mangrove mapping and hurricane-impact assessments [7,10]. This classifier identifies mangroves and exposed soil more accurately than support vector machine and neural network classifiers [10] and was successfully applied to detect mangrove damage following Hurricane Irma in Rookery Bay [7]. All technical and data reports, datasets, metadata, and data visualizations for both projects can be found in Office for Coastal Management reports [13,14]. 
For this study, the analysis of the land-cover maps was restricted to areas with consistent spatial coverage throughout the available time series and documented hurricane impacts, which included the southeastern region of Rookery Bay NERR (2017-2023, Figure A1) and the area surrounding Jobos Bay (2017-2024, Figure A2, and Figure 1c,d, red polygons). The southeastern region of Rookery Bay was the reserve area with more storm damage (Figure A1); however, due to lack of available satellite images for 2023, only the southernmost region of the reserve was used for this analysis (Figure 1a,b, red polygon). Because this restriction is driven jointly by image availability and by the location of documented damage, the analyzed domain is not a random sample of either reserve. At Rookery Bay in particular it is concentrated in the southern and southeastern portion of the reserve, which lay on the right side of Hurricane Irma’s track and sustained the greatest damage, so quantities derived from it are expected to differ systematically from reserve-wide values and are reported as such throughout (Section 3.2.2). The 2017 maps were derived from imagery acquired prior to hurricane landfall in September (Table A1). This represents pre-storm conditions. Subsequent annual maps were used to quantify hurricane-induced disturbance and evaluate ecosystem recovery.
Satellite imagery and preprocessing: Habitat maps were generated from very-high-resolution (~2 m), 8‑band multispectral WorldView‑2 and WorldView‑3 imagery provided by the NASA Commercial Smallsat Data Acquisition Program [26]. Level‑1B images were orthorectified, atmospherically corrected, and converted to remote‑sensing reflectance following McCarthy et al. [16], using a Digital Elevation Model (Copernicus GLO-30; [27]) and standard GDAL/MATLAB workflows [28,29] on a high‑performance computing cluster. Residual georeferencing errors, particularly in Jobos Bay, were reduced by manually aligning reflectance images to high‑resolution coastline vectors from NOAA’s Continually Updated Shoreline Product (CUSP, [30]). This alignment was performed visually, and no residual positional error statistics were computed for the archived products. At 2-m resolution a residual offset of a single pixel is sufficient to move the boundary between mangrove, soil, and water along a narrow fringe, so apparent transitions confined to the outermost one or two pixels of a shoreline should be treated with caution; this is the principal reason we do not interpret small differences in the coastal-water results. [AUTHOR ACTION: quantify interannual co-registration using independent control points, report positional RMSE by reserve and year, and test the sensitivity of the coastal-water trajectories to one- and two-pixel displacements of the annual maps.]  
Annual land-cover maps: Land-cover classification followed McCarthy et al. [15,16], using spectral indices and a decision-tree algorithm implemented in MATLAB without in situ training data. Classes were grouped into vegetated and non-vegetated types (Table A2), with mangroves distinguished from upland vegetation, low-relief vegetation, marsh, and unvegetated surfaces. Cloud and shadow pixels were explicitly classified, allowing partial use of cloud-contaminated scenes [15]. 
Annual mosaics were produced by combining classified scenes using the pixel-wise mode of class values using imagery acquired between January and April to minimize atmospheric variability; otherwise, the best available scenes were selected (Table A1). Cloud and shadow classes were merged into a no-data category in the annual composites (Table A2). Mosaicking was conducted in Google Earth Engine [31]. A complete list of image acquisitions and processing details is provided in the project Dataset Description [32]. Acquisition timing was not uniform across years and reserves (Table A1). Most mosaics draw on January-April imagery, but several depart substantially from that window, and the number of contributing acquisitions ranges from a single pass to several dozen. A pixel-wise mode taken over windows of different length, season, and observation count is not automatically comparable between years, particularly for water extent, shadow, and the seasonal contrast among mangrove, low vegetation, and soil; the NDVI seasonal amplitude measured at these sites (0.07-0.12; Section 3.3) indicates the magnitude of the phenological signal that off-season imagery could introduce into a classification. [AUTHOR ACTION: repeat the trajectory analysis restricted to a common seasonal window and, where feasible, to comparable scene counts or single-scene subsets, and report whether the pathway percentages change.]  
Map development incorporated iterative input from stakeholders at both reserves, including managers, advisory committees, and technical collaborators, to improve ecological realism and management relevance. Stakeholders contributed local ecological knowledge and management priorities to define regions of interest, refine classification rules, and review intermediate and final products. This process revealed georeferencing inconsistencies addressed by manual alignment, artifacts from the original maximum-value mosaicking approach [16] addressed by adopting mode-based compositing, and confusion between mangroves and upland vegetation in Jobos Bay addressed by adjusting decision-tree thresholds.
Post-processing combined temporal prevalence and spatial filters to reduce misclassifications. For each pixel, we computed the percentage of years assigned to each land-cover class and reclassified rare, context-inconsistent assignments (e.g., vegetation or mangrove classes occurring infrequently within pixels classified as water in most years) as no-data. For vegetation classes, prevalence criteria were combined with spatial masks derived from expert-defined polygons and existing habitat maps to indicate where mangroves were expected or unlikely to occur. Suspected mangrove-upland confusion was removed by reclassifying unlikely mangrove pixels outside known mangrove areas or with low mangrove prevalence as no-data. In Rookery Bay, pixels outside existing habitat maps were revised using temporal prevalence alone. Stakeholder input, obtained through repeated project meetings at both reserves, improved spatial consistency, classification accuracy, and ecological realism in the final land-cover time series. Two properties of this post-processing bear directly on the trajectory analysis and are stated here rather than deferred to the limitations. First, the temporal-prevalence rules use the full multi-year record, so each annual map depends in part on later years; the maps are therefore not independent between years, and a genuinely rare transition can be removed by the same rule that removes a rare misclassification. Second, the expert-defined masks encode prior expectation about where mangroves can occur, which suppresses exactly the kind of unexpected change that a disturbance study aims to detect. The exact rules, thresholds, and order of operations should be archived with the analysis code.]
Due to limited imagery, maps were not produced for either reserve in 2019. Availability of WorldViewTM images for Rookery Bay decreased after 2020. The 2023 mosaic of images only covered the southern portion of the reserve and there were no images available for 2024 (Figure A1). Coverage at Jobos Bay remained sufficient for annual maps throughout 2024 (Figure A2).
Map validation: Annual land-cover maps were validated using georeferenced field observations and expert visual interpretation of very-high-resolution base imagery available in Google Earth Pro. Field data were obtained from multi-year surveys at both reserves, including pre- and post-hurricane campaigns in 2014, 2018, and assessments of mangrove forest structure in 133 100 m2 plots at both reserves as part of the NERRS-MCC project in 2022-2023 (Ogurcak et al. in preparation). To avoid mixing pre‑ and post‑disturbance conditions in accuracy assessment, validation points within hurricane-affected areas were excluded because land cover at those locations changed after storm impact. Where field coverage was incomplete, additional validation points were assigned via visual interpretation by analysts familiar with local conditions to improve spatial representation. 
Three properties of this reference design constrain what the resulting metrics can demonstrate, and they should be carried through to the interpretation of every result below. First, the exclusion of validation points in hurricane-affected areas means that the reference sample deliberately avoids the locations where land cover changed, which are precisely the locations that drive the trajectory analysis presented here; the metrics therefore describe map quality in stable areas and cannot be extrapolated to the accuracy with which mangrove-to-soil, mangrove-to-water, or soil-to-mangrove transitions were detected in disturbed areas. Second, the same reference set is reused across years within each region, so the annual metrics are not statistically independent, and the reference labels are not matched in time to each annual map. Third, the reference labels combine field observations from a small number of campaigns with visual interpretation of base imagery whose acquisition dates differ from those of the maps being evaluated. Estimating the accuracy of the transitions themselves, and producing accuracy-adjusted area estimates, would require a reference sample that is temporally matched to each annual map, that contains both stable and changed pixels, and that is stratified by the principal transition types, following the good-practice recommendations of Olofsson et al. [60]. In the absence of one, the trajectories must remain presented as provisional mapped-area patterns, as they are in Sections 3.2 and 4.] 
For each annual map, the classified land-cover value at each validation point was extracted and compared with the corresponding reference class. Land-cover classes in both the maps and validation dataset were aggregated into mangrove and non-mangrove categories to focus accuracy assessment on mangrove detection. We calculated Prevalence, Accuracy, Balanced Accuracy, Matthews Correlation Coefficient, Recall/Sensitivity, Precision, F1-score, Critical Success Index, and Specificity. General definitions provided in Table A3 (see [33] for standard performance metric formulations). Validation data were used solely for accuracy assessment and not in classifier training or map generation. Because both the maps and the reference labels are collapsed to mangrove versus non-mangrove, Table A3 evaluates mangrove detection only. It does not evaluate the separation among natural non-vegetated soil, mixed low-relief vegetation, coastal water, inland water, and upland vegetation, even though those distinctions underpin Tables 1-3 and the mechanistic interpretation of the recovery pathways. Mangrove change trajectory analysis: Mangrove change following Hurricanes Irma and Maria was examined using pixel-wise land-cover succession analysis for Rookery Bay (2018-2023) and Jobos Bay (2018-2024). To simplify interpretation, original classes were consolidated into broader categories (e.g., grouping several low-vegetation classes into mixed low-relief vegetation and merging sand into soil), and developed surfaces were excluded (Table A2). Trajectory analyses were constrained to a “mangrove target area” comprising pixels classified as mangrove in 2017 that retained valid classifications across all subsequent annual mosaics (Figure 2). Within this domain, we quantified annual class composition by calculating the proportion of pixels in each land-cover class, providing time series of class abundance for mangrove, unvegetated, and other categories (Figures 3-4; Tables 1-2). Pixel-wise transition trajectories were derived by tracking each pixel’s annual class assignment through time, yielding multi-year class sequences such as mangrove loss shown by pixel conversion to other classes in 2018, followed by recovery of mangrove or conversion to other classes during the study period. Dominant temporal pathways of disturbance and recovery were aggregated and expressed as percentages of the mangrove target area (Figures 5, S2-S5; Table 3), providing metrics of resistance, recovery, and prolonged degradation.
Terminology is used in the following operational sense throughout. Resistance is the proportion of the mangrove target area still classified as mangrove in the 2018 (immediately post-storm) map. First return to mangrove is the earliest post-2018 map year in which a pixel is again classified as mangrove; because no map exists for 2019, the earliest resolvable return is 2020 and the true year of return for those pixels may be 2019 or 2020. Final mangrove status is the class in the last available map (2023 for Rookery Bay, 2024 for Jobos Bay). Recovery, unless qualified, means classification recovery: the pixel is mapped as mangrove in the target year, irrespective of intervening classes. A recurrent transition is a sequence containing two or more class changes after 2018, and persistent degradation describes a pixel not classified as any vegetated class in any map from 2020 onward. All of these are map-based definitions describing the canopy class assigned to a 2-m pixel; none of them, on its own, establishes recovery of forest structure, biomass, species composition, or ecosystem function (Section 4.7).
Restricting the analysis to pixels with valid classifications in every mosaic (complete-case selection) removes pixels that were cloud-obscured, shadowed, or masked in post-processing in any single year. These pixels are not randomly distributed, and they are plausibly more frequent in the most disturbed and most spectrally heterogeneous areas, so the target areas are biased toward consistently interpretable pixels and the resulting estimates of persistent degradation are likely to be conservative. Because of the complete-case requirement and the restricted spatial domain, every percentage reported below is a percentage of the analyzed 2017 mangrove target area, and not of the full pre-storm mangrove extent of either reserve.
Transition to water pixels were observed in the 2018 images in both coastal and inland settings within the mangrove target areas (Figure 2). Water transitions along the coast indicate shoreline erosion, persistent inundation, or collapse of fringe mangroves. Transitions to water farther from the coast may reflect localized flooding, groundwater changes, altered drainage, or impoundment. To distinguish between these different processes, we generated a perennial water mask from the annual maps available for 2014-2023 (seven mosaics per reserve, listed in Table A1; no map exists for 2019, and where more than one product exists for a year the mosaic used in the trajectory analysis was used), defining as perennial open water those pixels classified as water in at least six of those seven mosaics. [AUTHOR ACTION: confirm the exact years and products used at each site and list them explicitly here, since the map inventory differs between reserves.] Small inland water bodies were manually removed from the perennial water mask. A permanent open-water mask was then obtained by applying morphological erosion using a disk-shaped structuring element (5-pixel radius) to reduce small-scale coastal variability. We calculated the distance to the permanent open-water mask boundary and applied a 20 m threshold (ten 2-m resolution pixels) to separate waters directly connected to the open coast from interior water bodies. Pixels within 20 m of the perennial water boundary were classified as “coastal water”, whereas those farther away were classified as “inland water”. The three thresholds in this procedure — six of seven years of water classification, a 5-pixel (10 m) erosion radius, and the 20 m coastal/inland distance cut — together with the manual removal of small inland water bodies, were set by visual inspection of the resulting masks and are not independently calibrated. 2.3. Satellite Time Series Analysis
To characterize disturbance, degradation, and recovery at finer temporal resolution, satellite surface-reflectance time series were constructed for polygons representing three mangrove condition classes: less-impacted, recovered, and non-recovered stands. These areas were identified by comparing NERRS-MCC land-cover maps from 2017 with 2018 and subsequent years and by visually interpreting interannual land-cover transitions (Figure 2). The polygons are therefore delineated from the same land-cover maps, and from the same later years, that define the recovery categories analyzed in Section 3.2. The resulting NDVI series are descriptive characterizations of how those mapped categories behave spectrally through time; they are not independent validation of the classification or of the recovery categories, and they are not used as such anywhere in this paper. [AUTHOR ACTION: report the number, total area, and size distribution of the polygons in each condition class at each reserve, their geometry and selection criteria, and their pre-storm mean NDVI and variability; and show the between-polygon spread (for example the interquartile range across polygons) alongside the pooled means in Figures 6-8 and 10, so that within-class heterogeneity is visible — this matters most for the non-recovered class, whose polygons contain mixtures of mangrove, soil, and water.]
Many non-recovered mangrove patches were approximately 100 m across or smaller, which would produce mixed pixels in coarser sensors. We therefore used Sentinel-2 imagery, which provides 10-20 m spatial resolution, 13 spectral bands from visible to shortwave-infrared, and a short revisit time [34,35]. Sentinel-2 Level-2A surface-reflectance data (COPERNICUS/S2_SR_HARMONIZED, [36]) were obtained from the data catalog of Google Earth Engine 
Cloud, cloud-shadow, thin-cloud, and cloud-edge contamination was removed using the Cloud Score+ product [37], accessed through the Google Earth Engine collection GOOGLE/CLOUD_SCORE_PLUS/V1/S2_HARMONIZED, retaining only observations whose clear-pixel score exceeded a fixed threshold. [AUTHOR ACTION: state which Cloud Score+ band was used (cs or cs_cdf) and the exact clear-pixel threshold applied, and confirm that reference [37] now points to the Cloud Score+ product rather than to the earlier s2cloudless cloud-probability product.] Sentinel-2 bands acquired at 20 m and 60 m were resampled to the 10 m grid before any band arithmetic, so that all bands could be combined. [AUTHOR ACTION: state the resampling method used (for example nearest neighbour or bilinear).]
For each cloud-screened satellite pass, reflectance in each band was normalized at pixel level by dividing by the sum of reflectance across all bands of that sensor, and the normalized values were then averaged within the polygons of each mangrove condition class. The purpose of this closure normalization is to suppress residual pass-to-pass variability in illumination and atmospheric correction that remains in the Level-2A product, while preserving the relative shape of the spectrum (Figure S1). NDVI was computed from the polygon-averaged normalized bands, using B8 (near-infrared) and B4 (red) for Sentinel-2, as NDVI = (NIR − Red) / (NIR + Red) [38].
Two properties of this workflow should be stated explicitly, because normalization before band ratioing is unusual. First, NDVI is itself a normalized ratio, so at pixel level the closure normalization cancels exactly: dividing the near-infrared and the red band of a pixel by the same across-band sum leaves that pixel’s NDVI unchanged. The normalization therefore cannot introduce a sensor-dependent bias into pixel-level NDVI, and cannot by itself create or shift a temporal trend. Second, it does change the polygon value, because the spatial average is formed over normalized bands before the ratio is taken; this weights each pixel by the inverse of its across-band sum and so downweights bright pixels such as exposed soil and sunglint within a polygon. That behaviour is intended for tracking canopy condition in mixed polygons, but it means the reported series are not identical to the spatial average of pixel-level NDVI, and the difference grows with within-polygon brightness heterogeneity — that is, it is largest in the non-recovered class. Because Sentinel-2 coverage at Jobos Bay begins in late 2018 and did not capture the immediate post-hurricane period after September 2017, Landsat 8 and 9 imagery (30 m resolution) were used to extend the time series. Landsat revisit time ranged from 16 days (Landsat 8 only) to 8 days after the launch of Landsat 9, and the Operational Land Imager acquires visible to shortwave-infrared bands [39]. Landsat Level-2 Collection 2 Tier 1 surface-reflectance data (LANDSAT/LC08/C02/T1_L2 and LANDSAT/LC09/C02/T1_L2 [40,41]) were converted to physical reflectance using the Collection 2 Level-2 scaling coefficients (multiplicative factor 0.0000275, additive offset −0.2) before any further processing. Observations were screened with QA_PIXEL (discarding pixels flagged as cloud, dilated cloud, cirrus, cloud shadow, or snow), with QA_RADSAT (discarding pixels saturated in any band used), and with SR_QA_AEROSOL (discarding pixels flagged as high aerosol). The screened data were then processed with the same closure normalization and polygon averaging as Sentinel-2, and NDVI was calculated from the polygon-averaged normalized B5 (near-infrared) and B4 (red) bands. Inter-sensor agreement was assessed by pairing Landsat and Sentinel-2 observations of the same polygon within the same calendar month over the 2019-2025 overlap; monthly pairing rather than same-day pairing was necessary because cloud screening leaves few exactly coincident acquisitions. No cross-sensor correction was applied. 3. Results
3.1. Classification accuracy of annual land-cover maps
Annual habitat maps showed consistently high and temporally stable classification performance for both reserves (Table A3). In Rookery Bay, mangrove prevalence in the validation data was relatively low (0.22-0.61; mean 0.27), yet overall and balanced accuracy both averaged 0.93, and the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) was 0.83, indicating strong classification skill. Mangrove detection was robust, with recall, precision, and F1 scores of 0.94, 0.82, and 0.87, respectively, and specificity of 0.93 for non‑mangrove areas. In Jobos Bay, where mangrove prevalence was higher (0.60-0.70; mean 0.67), performance was similarly strong and stable. Overall and balanced accuracy both averaged 0.92, MCC averaged 0.83, and mangrove detection showed high recall (0.92), precision (0.96), and F1 score (0.94). Specificity remained high (0.93), indicating reliable discrimination of non‑mangrove pixels despite mangrove dominance.
These accuracy levels are consistent with recent mangrove mapping studies reporting overall accuracies of 0.85-0.97 and F1 scores ≥0.8 [42–44], and they are stable between years, which supports the use of the annual maps for analyzing interannual variability in mapped mangrove extent.
Two qualifications apply to these numbers and are carried through the remainder of the paper. The first concerns precision at Rookery Bay. There, a high overall accuracy (0.93) coexists with a noticeably lower precision (PPV 0.82) and F1 score (0.87), and the gap is widest in 2018 (ACC 0.92, F1 0.84, PPV 0.78). Because mangrove prevalence in the Rookery Bay reference sample is low (mean 0.27), overall accuracy is dominated by correctly identified non-mangrove points, whereas precision is not: a PPV of 0.82 implies that roughly one in five locations mapped as mangrove was not mangrove in the reference data. The widening of that gap in 2018 is consistent with post-storm canopy condition, when thinned and partly defoliated stands, exposed understory, and wet soil are spectrally closer to intact mangrove than in undisturbed years. False-positive mangrove in the immediate post-storm map would cause the 2018 map to overstate surviving mangrove, which would make the resistance estimate for Rookery Bay (46% of the target area) an upper bound and the subsequent recovery increments correspondingly conservative. At Jobos Bay, where mangrove prevalence is high (mean 0.67), precision is higher (0.96) and recall lower (0.92), so any corresponding bias runs in the opposite direction. [AUTHOR ACTION: add the class-specific accuracy metrics and full confusion matrices as Supplementary Table S1 and refer to them here.]
The second qualification is more consequential. These metrics were computed from a reference sample that excludes hurricane-affected locations and that aggregates all classes into mangrove versus non-mangrove (Section 2.2). They describe how reliably the maps identify mangrove where land cover did not change. They do not establish the accuracy of the mangrove-to-soil, mangrove-to-water, and soil-to-mangrove transitions on which the trajectory analysis rests, nor of the distinctions among soil, low vegetation, coastal water, inland water, and upland vegetation used in Tables 1-3. High accuracy in stable locations is not evidence of low uncertainty in changed locations. Accordingly, the trajectory results in Section 3.2 are reported as mapped-area patterns without confidence intervals, and should be read as provisional in that respect. 
Within the 2017 mangrove study areas (Figure 2), the occurrence of class 9 (upland vegetation) occurred in subsequent annual maps but remained rare, and given the short time frame and site conditions, these occurrences are most consistent with classification error rather than true conversion. In both study areas, class 9 represented less than 2% of the mapped area in every annual map, averaging 0.7 ± 0.6% in Rookery Bay (2018-2023) and 1.3 ± 0.4% in Jobos Bay (2018-2024). These consistently low percentages indicate that misclassification of mangrove as upland vegetation was infrequent within the target areas. This is an internal consistency check computed from the maps themselves rather than an independent accuracy estimate, and it speaks only to this one confusion pair.
3.2. Spatial mangrove disturbance and recovery
3.2.1. Land cover post-storm changes
Hurricanes Irma and Maria in September 2017 produced clear impacts in the 2018 habitat maps for both reserves (Figures A1-A2), with extensive areas of mangrove forest transitioning to non‑mangrove classes. In contrast, the 2023 maps show little evidence of change following Hurricanes Ian and Fiona in September 2022.
The 2017 habitat maps, based on pre‑storm imagery, were used as baselines because mangrove extent matched that observed in 2014-2016. The 2018 maps, derived from imagery acquired in the first four months of the year, show widespread shifts from mangrove to the natural non‑vegetated soil class, consistent with field observations of defoliation and structural damage (broken branches, snapped trunks, uprooted trees). Loss of green canopy exposed the ground layer in the satellite imagery, so affected mangrove stands were classified as the underlying substrate visible after the storm (Figure 2). Many pixels that were mangrove in 2017 shifted to soil in 2018; others changed to low vegetation, consistent with exposed understory; and fringe mangrove pixels converted to water, indicating canopy loss over water and/or shoreline retreat. In subsequent years, some affected areas gradually reverted to mangrove (Figures A1-A2), revealing spatially heterogeneous recovery, whereas other regions remained non‑mangrove in 2023-2024. We quantified those changes using pixel-wise trajectories. 
3.2.2. Mangrove Recovery Pathways
A pixel-wise land-cover succession analysis was used to characterize annual class transitions and mangrove recovery trajectories. Transition pathways were derived by tracking each pixel’s class sequence through time within the mangrove target areas defined for each region (Figure 2). To simplify the analysis, trajectories were evaluated using a reduced set of classes (Table A2): mangrove, upland vegetation, mixed low-relief vegetation, natural non-vegetated soil, and water.
A key constraint on interpreting recovery timing is the absence of annual maps for 2019 in both reserves. The 2017 maps represent pre-storm conditions, and the 2018 maps capture immediate post-storm disturbance, but the next annual maps are from 2020. As a result, some areas mapped as “recovery” by 2020 may have recovered during 2019.
The time-series length also differed between reserves. In Rookery Bay, reduced spatial coverage in 2023 limited the target area to the southern portion of the reserve, and 2024 coverage was insufficient for annual mapping. Therefore, recovery pathways in Rookery Bay were evaluated over five annual maps (2018-2020-2021-2022-2023). In Jobos Bay, more complete coverage allowed inclusion of 2024, yielding six annual maps (2018-2020-2021-2022-2023-2024). Because the Jobos Bay time series includes one additional year and thus a larger number of possible trajectories, pathway results are reported as percentages rather than counts of unique paths to allow direct comparison between the reserves.
For the whole area of the land-cover maps (blue polygons in Figure 1), mangrove extent in 2017 totaled 307.0 km² in Rookery Bay and 9.6 km² in Jobos Bay, derived from pixel counts using latitude-corrected pixel area. The mangrove target areas actually analyzed — pixels classified as mangrove in 2017 that also retained valid classifications in every subsequent mosaic — were 4,298.5 ha (43.0 km²) at Rookery Bay and 620.4 ha (6.2 km²) at Jobos Bay (Tables 1 and 2). They therefore represent 14.0% and 64.6% of the 2017 mapped mangrove extent at each site. The difference reflects the reduced spatial coverage at Rookery Bay, where the analysis is restricted to the southern and southeastern portion of the reserve, and the more complete coverage at Jobos Bay. The mapped domains, and hence the target areas, include mangroves surrounding the reserves and are not limited to the NERR boundaries. [AUTHOR ACTION: an earlier version of this manuscript reported these fractions as 24.2% and 70.0%, which is inconsistent with the target-area values in Tables 1 and 2; verify which quantities are correct and ensure the chosen values are propagated to the Abstract, Highlights, Discussion, Conclusions, figures, tables, and Supplement.]
Two consequences follow, and they apply to every percentage reported in this section. First, all trajectory percentages are percentages of these target areas, not of the pre-storm mangrove extent of either reserve, and the phrase “percent of pre-storm extent” is avoided throughout. Second, the Rookery Bay target area is not a random subsample of that reserve: it is concentrated in the sector that lay on the right side of Hurricane Irma’s track and sustained the most damage (Figure A1), so its disturbance statistics are expected to be more severe, and its recovery statistics less representative, than reserve-wide values would be. The Jobos Bay target area, covering about two-thirds of the mapped 2017 extent there, is closer to representative of that site. Comparisons between the two sites below should accordingly be read as comparisons between these two unequally sampled domains rather than between the reserves in their entirety. 
Results are summarized in Figures 3-5 and Tables 1-3. Figures 3-4 show annual class composition integrated within each mangrove target area. Figure 5 (with detailed views in Figures S2-S5) illustrates alluvial diagrams of class transitions for pixels classified as mangrove in 2017. Tables 1-2 present percentages of pixels transitioning from their original 2017 or 2018 class to each mapped class in target years integrated by mangrove target area; the first sections focus on 2017 mangroves, and subsequent rows describe dynamics of classes that appeared in 2018. Table 3 lists the most common annual class pathways for affected mangroves at the pixel level. Most trajectories involved transitions among mangrove, low vegetation, soil, and water classes, whereas only a small fraction resulted in persistent transitions away from mangrove. 
Within the mangrove target areas, the fraction of pixels still classified as mangrove fell to 46% at Rookery Bay and 59% at Jobos Bay in the 2018 maps. It then rose to 90% at Rookery Bay by 2023 and to 83% at Jobos Bay by 2024 (Tables 1 and 2, Figures 2-4 and A1-A2). Because the two series end in different years, the like-for-like comparison is for 2023, when 90.5% of the Rookery Bay target area and 78.9% of the Jobos Bay target area were classified as mangrove (Tables 1 and 2); the 83% figure for Jobos Bay includes a further year of recovery for which no Rookery Bay counterpart exists. In contrast, no change in mapped mangrove extent comparable to the 2017 signal was observed after Hurricane Ian at Rookery Bay or Hurricane Fiona at Jobos Bay in 2022 (Figures A1-A2). Because no uncertainty estimate is attached to these mapped areas (Section 3.1), differences are described throughout as larger or smaller rather than as statistically significant, and no inferential test is implied. 
In what follows, “recovery” denotes classification recovery as defined in Section 2.2, that is, a pixel being mapped as the mangrove class again; it does not by itself imply recovery of forest structure, biomass, or species composition (Section 4.7). The terms “low vegetation” and “short vegetation” are used interchangeably for the mixed low-relief vegetation class (Table A2). For both reserves, areas that transitioned to short vegetation in 2018 showed the highest subsequent return to the mangrove class, whereas areas that transitioned to water showed the lowest. When mangrove recovery was expressed as the percentage of the area occupied by pixels that had changed class in the 2018 maps, Rookery Bay showed the highest recovery by 2023 for pixels that transitioned to short vegetation (91.9%), followed by those that transitioned to soil (84.9%), while pixels that transitioned to water showed the lowest recovery (51.5%). A similar pattern was observed in Jobos Bay by 2024, although recovery percentages were lower: 85.5% for short vegetation, 67.9% for soil, and 39.4% for water. 
3.2.2.1. Mangrove Recovery Pathways at Rookery Bay
2018 Mangrove class: Nearly half of the 2017 mangrove pixels remained classified as mangrove in 2018 (46%; Figure 3, Table 1). Most of these areas (83%) persisted as mangrove through 2020-2023 (Table 3). A small subset of this group (<7%) showed brief (1 year) transitions to short vegetation in subsequent years before returning to mangrove, a pattern consistent with temporary visibility of understory vegetation. 
2018 Soil class: Over 45% of 2017 mangrove pixels were reclassified as bare soil in 2018 (Table 1), especially along the eastern eyewall path of Hurricane Irma (Figures 1, A1). These areas exhibited a wide range of disturbance and recovery trajectories (Table 3, Figure 5, Figure S2). By 2023, 84.9% of these soil pixels were again classified as mangrove (Table 1). Of the 2018 soil area, 80.8 percentage points are accounted for by the dominant pathways enumerated in Table 3, which fall into two main mechanisms; the remaining recovery occurred through pathways each representing less than 1% of the class and therefore not listed individually. The first, and most common recovery pathway, was a direct transition from soil to mangrove (69.2% of pixels across six paths), with most recovery occurring rapidly by 2020 (54.5%) and smaller fractions recovering in 2021 (5.6%) and 2022 (4.9%). The second mechanism involved a transition through short vegetation before mangrove recovery (11.6% of pixels across seven paths), typically with a one-year intermediate state. 
A small fraction of soil pixels followed more complex trajectories involving short vegetation or mangrove but did not end as mangrove by 2023 (3.1%; five paths). Nearly 6% of soil pixels never transitioned to any vegetation class, remaining soil or oscillating between soil and water across the five post-storm annual maps. 
2018 Low Vegetation class: A smaller portion of mangrove pixels (<7%) transitioned to mixed low-relief vegetation in 2018 (Table 1), consistent with exposed understory or short vegetation visible after canopy loss. By 2023, almost 92% of these pixels had returned to mangrove (Table 1, Figure S2), primarily via 3 recovery paths (Table 3). The dominant pathway was ‘rapid recovery to mangrove’ by 2020 (sequence 13-10-10-10-10; 74%), consistent with moderate canopy damage and fast regrowth. Other paths (~12.5%) involved persistence or temporary return to other vegetation before mangrove recovery in 2023, Only 0.1% of 2018 low-vegetation pixels converted to soil or water in 2020 and failed to return to any vegetation class in 2023.

3.2.2.2. Mangrove Recovery Pathways at Jobos Bay
Jobos Bay results are presented in direct comparison with Rookery Bay, focusing on percentages rather than counts of unique paths because the Jobos Bay time series includes one additional year, increasing the number of possible paths.
2018 mangrove class: In Jobos Bay, 58.6% of 2017 mangrove pixels remained classified as mangrove in 2018 (Figure 4, Table 2). Most of these areas (75.4%) persisted as mangrove through 2024 (Table 3). Approximately 13.6% of pixels showed temporary intrusions from other classes, and only 1.15% followed trajectories that did not return to mangrove by 2024, typically transitioning to soil with no subsequent return to any vegetated classes.
Mangrove coverage decreased in 2022 (Figure 4), resulting in the second-lowest mangrove area in the record after 2017. The areas no longer classified as mangrove were primarily reclassified as soil and low vegetation (Figure 4, Figure S4). Because the 2022 mosaic was generated using imagery acquired during the first four months of the year (Table A1), this decrease predates the September 2022 storm and therefore cannot be attributed to that event. 
2018 Soil class: Most mangrove pixels that became soil in 2018 returned to mangrove by 2024 (67.9%; Table 2, Figure 5), with nearly half recovering by 2020 (49.0%). However, a substantial fraction did not recover and remained as soil in 2024 (24.0%). As in Rookery Bay, the principal recovery mechanism was a direct transition from soil to mangrove (51.1%; Table 3, Figure S4), with most recovery occurring by 2020 (30.8%). Recovery via short vegetation was less frequent at Jobos Bay than at Rookery Bay (6.8%), with intermediate low-vegetation states typically lasting one year. Unlike Rookery Bay, a notable proportion of 2018 soil pixels at Jobos Bay did not return to mangrove or other vegetation (29.2%; Table 2, Figure S4). Many of these pixels (8.6%) remained soil throughout 2020-2024 (Table 3), while others oscillated between soil and water (4.4%) or had brief transitions to mangrove or low vegetation (3.9%). 
2018 Low Vegetation class: Similar to Rookery Bay, a small fraction of mangrove pixels (7.4%; Table 2, Figure 4) transitioned to mixed low-relief vegetation in 2018, consistent with exposed understory vegetation. Of these, 74.1% reverted to mangrove by 2020, increasing to 85.5% by 2024 (Table 2, Figure S4), which is lower than at Rookery Bay (89% by 2020 and 91.9% by 2023; Table 1). This is consistent with a notable fraction of low-vegetation pixels remaining as low vegetation in 2020 (9.6%) or transitioning to soil (13.2% in 2020; 7.8% in 2024). 
By 2024, 85.5% of 2018 low-vegetation pixels had returned to mangrove, mostly via 11 recovery paths. The most common trajectories involved fast recovery to mangrove by 2020 (two paths, 62.0%; Table 3), with an additional 7% recovering by 2021. However, 14.5% did not return to mangrove by 2024 (Figure S4), including a dominant path in which low vegetation converted to soil (2.2% of pixels). A small proportion ended in other vegetation classes (5.8%), predominantly short vegetation.

3.3. Higher-temporal-resolution analysis of disturbance and recovery
Annual land-cover maps reveal storm-driven mangrove disturbance and recovery, but they cannot resolve shorter-term dynamics or seasonal variability. To capture these finer changes, high-temporal-resolution NDVI time series were analyzed for mangrove areas representing three post-storm response types: (a) less-impacted mangroves, (b) impacted but recovered mangroves, and (c) non-recovered mangroves. NDVI was derived from single-date Sentinel-2 passes for both reserves and complemented with Landsat 8/9 imagery to extend the early record at Jobos Bay. NDVI climatologies were calculated from less-impacted regions, excluding data within one year after the storm, to avoid contamination by immediate disturbance. Similarly to the analysis of the land-cover maps, NDVI detected the effect of the 2017 storms but not the 2022 storms. Two points about interpretation apply to all of Section 3.3. First, the condition polygons were delineated from the same annual land-cover maps, including the later years used to decide whether an area had recovered (Section 2.3), so the NDVI series describe the spectral behaviour of the mapped categories rather than independently validating them. Second, recovery-onset dates quoted below (November 2017 at Jobos Bay, June 2018 in southern Rookery Bay) were identified from the monthly-mean series by inspection. 4. Discussion
4.1. What the classification accuracy does and does not support
Our time series of annual land-cover maps provided the basis for quantifying changes in mapped mangrove canopy cover following major hurricanes in two contrasting reserves, and classification accuracies matched or exceeded those reported in recent regional and global mangrove mapping studies [9,42–44,46,47]. The scope of that assessment must be stated precisely, however, because it sets the limits of everything that follows. The metrics in Table A3 evaluate a binary mangrove/non-mangrove decision, at reference locations deliberately chosen to exclude hurricane-affected areas, against a reference set reused across years. They support the statement that the annual maps identify mangrove reliably where land cover was stable. They do not, on their own, establish that the multi-year transitions among soil, low vegetation, coastal water, inland water, and mangrove reported in Tables 1-3 are accurately detected in the disturbed areas where those transitions occur. High accuracy in stable locations is not evidence of low uncertainty in changed locations, and we do not treat it as such.
The use of high-resolution imagery, decision-tree classification with regionally tuned thresholds, and iterative stakeholder feedback further reduced confusion between mangroves and spectrally similar upland vegetation. These methodological choices enabled robust identification of pixel-level disturbance and recovery trajectories, differentiation between inland and coastal water responses, and detection of spatially coherent areas of persistent canopy loss. This approach extends earlier work that applied the same methodology to mangrove mapping, assessing Hurricane Irma impacts in southwest Florida, and benchmarking the classifier against alternative machine-learning approaches [7,10,15,16].  
A critical indicator of reliability was the low recurrence of spectrally similar upland vegetation (class 9) within the 2017 mangrove footprint (Figure 2) which remained below 2% in every annual map. This is consistent with the independently validated recall, precision, and F1 scores reported above, and together the two lines of evidence indicate that the combination of a decision-tree classifier and map post-processing effectively distinguished mangroves from terrestrial vegetation, including under conditions of canopy thinning or defoliation. The credibility of the mapped disturbance signal is further supported by the spatially and temporally coherent transition of 25-45% of the 2017 mangrove area to exposed soil in 2018, consistent with hurricane-driven disturbance rather than random classification variability.
Several lines of internal evidence nonetheless argue that the dominant mapped patterns are not classification noise. The 2018 disturbance signal is spatially coherent and aligned with the storm tracks rather than randomly distributed; it is confined to the year following the storms; the recurrence of spectrally similar upland vegetation within the 2017 mangrove footprint stays below 2% in every year; the dominant pathways are short and directional (soil to mangrove, low vegetation to mangrove) rather than the oscillation that repeated independent misclassification would produce; and the mapped pattern is corroborated by field observations of defoliation, snapped stems, and subsequent resprouting at both reserves in 2018 and in 2022-2023. Repeated random classification error would not produce a signal with these properties, and a time sequence from soil through low vegetation to mangrove is therefore more plausibly read as damage followed by progressive recovery than as an artifact of varying image quality.
What this evidence cannot supply is a quantitative error bar. Each pixel sequence spans five or six maps, and classification error compounds across dates and classes; the effect is largest for the low-frequency paths and for the recurrent alternations between water, soil, low vegetation, and mangrove, which are precisely the trajectories of greatest management interest. Without confusion matrices matched in time and stratified by transition type, the pathway percentages in Tables 1-3 should be read as mapped-area proportions with unquantified uncertainty, and small differences between pathway classes or between reserves should not be interpreted as precise ecological quantities. Propagating annual class uncertainty through the multi-year sequences — for example by Monte Carlo resampling of each pixel’s annual class from temporally appropriate confusion matrices, combined with a perturbation representing residual co-registration error — would be the appropriate way to attach confidence intervals to mangrove cover, transition percentages, recovery proportions, and the dominant pathways. We identify this as the principal methodological priority for extending this work. 4.2. Disturbance was associated with local hurricane-force wind exposure
The contrasting responses to the 2017 and 2022 hurricanes are consistent with local exposure to hurricane-force winds being the dominant proximal control on widespread, remotely detectable mangrove disturbance at both reserves, rather than storm rainfall or surge alone. This is an observational contrast between four site-storm combinations, not a controlled comparison, and it is treated as such throughout this section. Hurricanes Irma and Maria produced extensive canopy loss, marked NDVI declines, and transitions from mangrove to soil or water classes, whereas Hurricanes Ian and Fiona produced no comparable signal in either the annual land-cover maps or the dense NDVI time series, and 2023 field surveys indicated minimal defoliation from winds. This contrast is notable because Ian and Fiona were major hurricanes and generated substantial hydrologic forcing at the study sites, including an approximately 7-ft storm surge in Rookery Bay during Ian [24] and 20-25 in of rainfall in Jobos Bay during Fiona [25]. Nevertheless, the reserves were exposed mainly to strong tropical-storm-force winds in 2022 (≥ 50 kt), rather than the hurricane-force winds experienced in 2017 (≥ 64 kt) [22–25]. 
Storm track relative to each reserve helps explain this difference. During Irma, the eyewall crossed central Rookery Bay NERR, placing the southern portion of Rookery Bay analyzed here on the right side of the storm track, where the combination of forward storm motion and cyclonic circulation generally produces stronger winds, greater surge, and more severe wind damage [48]. In contrast, Ian made landfall approximately 100 km north of Rookery Bay. Although Ian produced a substantial surge in the region, local winds over the reserve were considerably weaker than those associated with Irma. Similarly, Jobos Bay was approximately 40 km from Maria’s track in 2017 and experienced hurricane-force winds, whereas Fiona made landfall approximately 100 km west of the reserve in 2022, exposing the site to extreme rainfall but weaker winds. Thus, storm category at landfall alone did not determine local ecological disturbance. Instead, the local wind field and a reserve’s position relative to the storm track determined whether wind exposure was sufficient to generate widespread canopy damage.
Our results therefore suggest an apparent threshold-dependent response, in which widespread spectral evidence of disturbance emerged when local wind speeds reached hurricane force, but not when exposure remained below that level. This should not be interpreted as a universal 64-kt ecological threshold, because wind damage depends on storm duration and gust characteristics, species composition, stand height and canopy structure, antecedent rainfall and soil conditions, and local topographic and hydrogeomorphic setting [4,49]. Rather, in these two systems the cases in which widespread canopy damage was detected are also the cases with hurricane-force wind exposure. With two storms per site, local wind speed is confounded with storm track, season, antecedent canopy state, hydrology, geomorphology, prior disturbance, and image availability, and four site-storm combinations cannot by themselves identify a wind threshold or rank wind against rainfall and surge. The value of the present contrast is that it is consistent with, and adds two independently mapped sites to, studies that could make that separation with much larger samples: Imbert [49] found maximum mangrove basal-area loss at wind speeds ≥130 km h-1 (70 kt), and Taillie et al. [50] found maximum wind speed to be the strongest predictor of hurricane-related mangrove damage across the 2017 Atlantic season, with stronger effects than surge and rainfall, although flooding, forest structure, and prior disturbance also contributed. [AUTHOR ACTION: if a stronger attribution is to be retained anywhere in the manuscript, assemble site-specific wind, rainfall, surge, storm duration, and antecedent-condition variables and fit an explicit comparative model with uncertainty; otherwise the wording used here should be kept consistently.] 
The different signals observed by the two remote-sensing analyses are important for interpreting this result. The annual land-cover maps captured major structural disturbance, including canopy removal sufficient to expose the under-canopy class. The NDVI time series captured changes in green canopy condition, including defoliation and reduced photosynthetic activity that may occur without a persistent land-cover transition. Neither approach directly measures hydrologic stress, salinity change, soil redox conditions, root damage, or sublethal physiological stress. Consequently, the absence of a detectable 2022 signal does not demonstrate that Ian and Fiona had no ecological effects; hydrological, physiological, or delayed impacts would not necessarily appear in either indicator. It indicates only that no widespread canopy signal was detected at the spatial, temporal, and spectral resolution analyzed here.
This distinction helps explain why extreme rainfall and surge in 2022 did not produce a spectral response comparable to 2017. Storm surge, sediment transport, and severe rainfall can alter porewater salinity, hydroperiod, soil nutrients, soil oxygen availability, and redox conditions, but the effects on forest canopy may be delayed, spatially heterogeneous, or buffered where elevation, drainage, tidal exchange, and sediment accretion remain adequate [51,52]. In contrast, hurricane-force winds can cause immediate defoliation, branch breakage, stem failure, uprooting, and canopy collapse [2,8], producing abrupt changes in visible and near-infrared reflectance that are readily detected by NDVI and high-resolution land-cover classification [53]. The 2022 storms therefore provide a useful contrast rather than a controlled experiment: even with extreme rainfall at Jobos Bay and substantial surge at Rookery Bay, no concurrent widespread canopy signal was detected, which is what would be expected if wind exposure were the dominant proximal driver of the disturbance observed after the 2017 hurricanes. Establishing that attribution would require site-specific wind, rainfall, surge, duration, and antecedent-condition variables across a larger set of storms and sites, together with an explicit comparative model.
4.3. Return to the mangrove class was high but spatially heterogeneous
Despite the severity of the 2017 storm disturbances, most affected areas within the analyzed target areas were eventually mapped as mangrove again; that recovery was strongly heterogeneous in space and time. At both reserves, a substantial fraction of the 2017 mangrove target area remained classified as mangrove throughout the study period, indicating high resistance or rapid recovery in many stands. Among pixels that transitioned to non-mangrove classes in 2018, the majority were classified as mangrove again in the 2020 map, that is within two to three years of the storms; because no map exists for 2019, the year of first return cannot be resolved more finely than this, and some of these pixels may have recovered during 2019. Recovery was fastest where the initial post-storm class was bare soil or low vegetation. At Rookery Bay, the mangrove fraction of the target area declined to 46% in 2018, rose to 81% in 2020, and reached 90% in 2023; at Jobos Bay it declined to 59% in 2018, rose to 73% in 2020, reached 79% in 2023, and 83% in 2024. Similar recovery times have been observed in Florida Everglades mangroves, where aboveground carbon stocks lost during hurricanes were largely recovered within approximately four years [6]. A smaller but non-trivial proportion of spatially clustered pixels exhibited persistent degradation and limited recovery, particularly along coastal fringes and in both hydrologically altered inland locations or within lower elevation basin forests. These patterns underscore that while mangrove forests in the areas analyzed at these two reserves regained canopy cover to a substantial degree after severe storms, recovery is not uniform or guaranteed, and local environmental conditions strongly modulate post-disturbance trajectories. Mangrove responses to hurricanes span a continuum from high resistance to delayed recovery and, in some locations, transition toward persistent, degraded states.
Our trajectory analysis revealed two dominant recovery mechanisms following severe canopy loss: direct transitions from bare soil to mangrove, and recovery from mixed low-relief vegetation to mangrove. The first mechanism, which accounted for most of the recovering area in both reserves, consisted of pixels remaining classified as bare soil for one or more years before transitioning directly to mangrove. The one-year transitions, the most common pathway, suggest rapid canopy recovery, consistent with damage that was primarily limited to canopy defoliation and minor branch loss rather than severe branch loss and widespread tree mortality. Longer transitions from bare soil to mangrove may reflect more severe canopy or structural damage, with recovery occurring through resprouting from surviving trees, including basal or epicormic shoots in the species Laguncularia racemosa and Avicennia germinans. Resprouting individuals of both species were commonly observed in the mangrove structural assessment conducted in both reserves (2022 - 2023). In Rookery Bay, transition of 2018 soil pixels through short vegetation before mangrove recovery (11.6%), possibly points to recruitment and establishment of mangrove propagules.
The second mechanism involved one or two years of mixed low-relief vegetation before transitioning to mangrove. Following canopy loss, the exposed surface likely included a mixture of understory vegetation including mangrove saplings and herbaceous succulents such as Batis maritima and Sesuvium portulacastrum, which were observed at the mangrove plots sampled post-hurricane. The relatively short duration of this intermediate stage (typically one to two years) suggests that these areas experienced either a combination of less severe impact and rapid recovery or recovery through resprouting and/or advance regeneration (i.e., seedlings and saplings that can take advantage of increased light levels following storm-induced canopy thinning) [5]. Overall, the predominance of short recovery pathways indicates that post-hurricane mangrove recovery was driven primarily by the recovery of surviving trees rather than by prolonged, multi-stage successional replacement.
Areas that transitioned from mangrove in 2017 to water in 2018 exhibited substantially lower rates of return to the mangrove class (51.5% at Rookery Bay by 2023 and 39.4% at Jobos Bay by 2024, expressed as percentages of the 2018 water class) than those transitioning to soil or low vegetation. These water classes are small in absolute terms (66.5 ha at Rookery Bay, 32.4 ha at Jobos Bay) and lie disproportionately along narrow fringes, where residual co-registration error at 2-m resolution has its largest effect (Section 4.8), so the contrast should be read as a robust ordering rather than as a precise pair of values. Lower mangrove recovery was particularly evident along exposed coastal fringes, where persistent water or soil states were more prevalent, indicating geomorphic and hydrologic changes such as shoreline erosion, prolonged inundation, storm-surge ponding, or loss of substrate elevation that can inhibit recolonization over ecologically relevant time scales. In Rookery Bay, red mangroves (Rhizophora mangle) dominate the shoreline and fringe zones and may be especially vulnerable to severe structural damage because recovery is limited to advance regeneration and propagule recruitment, as this species does not exhibit epicormic growth. Similar patterns in recovery failure were documented across southwest Florida (including the current study area) following Hurricane Irma using a combination of Landsat imagery and lidar derived DEM, highlighting the vulnerability of low elevation mangrove areas to ponding and prolonged inundation [8]. Similarly, lower mangrove recovery has been found in fringe mangrove, especially for Rhizophora mangle in northeast Caribbean [49]. In contrast, inland water pixels more frequently transitioned to soil or back to mangrove, suggesting that inland areas may experience sediment deposition or hydrologic adjustments that eventually allow vegetation re-establishment. Smith et al. [4] reported that some Everglades mangrove forests recovered after catastrophic hurricane disturbance, whereas others were permanently converted to mudflats, with damage severity varying among geomorphic settings. Coastal fringe zones in our study reserves appeared more vulnerable to persistent erosion, hydrologic alteration, and structural collapse of mangrove stands.
These contrasting trajectories also have important implications for blue-carbon storage and coastal protection. Areas that recovered structurally and functionally are likely to regain at least part of their carbon sequestration capacity and shoreline stabilization functions over decadal time scales [1,6]. In contrast, areas that transitioned to persistent water or non-vegetated soil may represent long-term reductions in carbon storage, diminished resistance to future storm surges, and possible shifts toward alternative ecosystem states such as mudflats or open water. Identifying these persistent degradation hotspots is therefore important for prioritizing hydrologic restoration, erosion mitigation, and long-term ecological monitoring within mangrove reserves.
Both regions showed examples of limited mangrove recovery associated with pre-existing stressors. In eastern Aguirre State Forest and along Camino del Indio, persistent canopy loss occurred in areas affected by impounded hydrology and development encroachment, respectively, suggesting that pre-existing environmental degradation reduced post-storm resilience. In Rookery Bay, an inland lagoon around Pumpkin Bay had documented mangrove die-offs in 1999 (Jill Schmid, personal communication). Although mangrove cover was present in 2017, this area showed little to no mangrove recovery following Hurricane Irma. Previous studies have likewise shown that mangrove forests affected by hydrologic alteration or other forms of degradation are less likely to recover naturally following major storm impacts [2,54]. Lewis et al. [55] describe the chronic stressors and mechanisms that can lead to mass mortality following acute disturbances such as hurricanes as mangrove heart-attacks.
4.4. Controls on the spatial heterogeneity of recovery
The trajectory analysis shows clearly which pixels returned to the mangrove class and which did not, but the maps alone cannot explain why. Three groups of controls are consistent with the observed spatial pattern, and each generates a prediction that the present data set does not yet test.
The first is the post-storm surface state itself, which acts as a proxy for damage severity. Pixels reclassified as low vegetation in 2018 recovered most (91.9% at Rookery Bay by 2023, 85.5% at Jobos Bay by 2024), pixels reclassified as soil recovered less (84.9% and 67.9%), and pixels reclassified as water recovered least (51.5% and 39.4%). This ordering is what would be expected if canopy thinning that exposes a vegetated understory reflects the least severe damage, exposure of bare substrate reflects loss of canopy over surviving or dead stems, and conversion to water reflects loss of the substrate itself or a change in hydroperiod that no longer supports establishment.
The second is hydrogeomorphic setting. Low recovery is concentrated along the exposed coastal fringe and in low-lying interior basins. Along the fringe, shoreline retreat, loss of substrate elevation, and continued wave exposure remove both the surviving root systems and the sediment surface required for propagule establishment; Lagomasino et al. [8] identified this combination of low elevation, impeded drainage, and surge deposits as the control on dieback extent in southwest Florida after Irma, using lidar-derived elevation. In the interior, impounded or tidally disconnected basins retain storm water, and prolonged inundation with low soil oxygen suppresses both resprouting and recruitment [51,52]. The two largest persistent-loss areas identified here, eastern Aguirre State Forest and the eastern portion of Camino del Indio, are both of this type, and the second is explicitly associated with infilling of a tidal channel by storm-surge deposits.
The third is species composition and regeneration mode. Rhizophora mangle, which dominates the fringe at Rookery Bay, does not resprout epicormically and can recover only through advance regeneration and propagule recruitment, whereas Avicennia germinans and Laguncularia racemosa resprout from basal and epicormic shoots and were observed doing so in the 2022-2023 structural plots at both reserves. Fringe zones therefore combine the most severe physical damage with the regeneration mode least able to respond to it, which is consistent both with the low recovery of fringe pixels found here and with the lower recovery of Rhizophora-dominated fringe reported for the northeastern Caribbean [49].
Separating these controls, and quantifying their relative importance, requires auxiliary layers that were not part of this analysis. [AUTHOR ACTION: overlay the pixel trajectories with lidar- or DEM-derived elevation, distance to the open coast, distance to the storm track, modelled peak wind, and available soil and hydrologic data, and fit a model of final mangrove status on these predictors. This would convert the mechanisms proposed above from plausible explanations into tested ones, and is the most direct way to strengthen the management relevance of the pathway results.]
4.5. Mangrove gain versus recovery of pre-existing mangroves
Because the trajectory analysis is defined on pixels that were mangrove in 2017, it measures the return of pre-existing mangrove and is structurally unable to detect mangrove establishment on surfaces that were not mangrove before the storms. The distinction matters for reading the recovery percentages: a target area that is 90% mangrove in 2023 has regained 90% of its own 2017 canopy cover, and says nothing about whether mangrove extent elsewhere in the mapped domain increased or decreased over the same period.
Areas of gain are nevertheless visible in the interannual difference maps. At Jobos Bay, the 2024 minus 2017 difference (Figure A3, bottom; Figures S7 and S8) shows patches in blue where mangrove was mapped in 2024 but not in 2017. These occur mainly along the landward margins of recovering stands and at the edges of interior basins, a distribution consistent with lateral expansion into space opened by the storm and with the maturation of seedlings and saplings recruited after 2017 into a canopy detectable at 2-m resolution. [AUTHOR ACTION: quantify this explicitly. For the full mapped domain at each reserve, report the area classified as non-mangrove in 2017 and as mangrove in the final year, broken down by 2017 class (soil, low vegetation, water, upland vegetation), in hectares and as a percentage of the 2017 mangrove extent. Distinguish pixels that were mangrove at some point in 2014-2016, were lost before 2017, and were regained (recovery on a longer baseline) from pixels with no mangrove anywhere in the record before 2018 (genuine gain). If net gain proves to be small, state the value and say so explicitly.]
Two caveats apply to any such estimate. Gain measured as the difference between two annual maps is the sum of true establishment and of classification difference between those maps, and at the scale of the blue areas in Figure A3 the two are of comparable magnitude, so the qualifications of Section 3.1 apply with more force here than to the large 2018 disturbance signal. In addition, apparent gain along shorelines is sensitive to residual co-registration error at 2-m resolution. Gain estimates should therefore carry the same accuracy caveats as the recovery estimates, and would ideally be supported by their own change-stratified reference sample.
4.6. Differences in recovery between the two sites
Although both reserves exhibited substantial recovery following the 2017 hurricanes, recovery of the mangrove class was less complete at Jobos Bay than at Rookery Bay. Compared in the same year, 90.5% of the Rookery Bay target area and 78.9% of the Jobos Bay target area were classified as mangrove in 2023; the Jobos Bay value had risen to 83% by 2024, for which no Rookery Bay map exists. Two properties of the analysis qualify this comparison. The Rookery Bay target area covers 14.0% of that reserve’s mapped 2017 mangrove extent and is drawn from its most damaged sector, whereas the Jobos Bay target area covers 64.6% of that site’s mapped extent; and the Rookery Bay series is one year shorter. The difference between 90.5% and 78.9% is therefore a difference between two unequally sampled domains, and should not be read directly as a difference in reserve-scale resilience. Jobos Bay also showed lower recovery of pixels that transitioned to soil or low vegetation in 2018, as well as a higher proportion of persistent non-vegetated trajectories. These differences may partly reflect the greater extent of pre-existing natural and anthropogenic stressors in Jobos Bay, in addition to differences in hydrology, geomorphic setting, local disturbance exposure, and climatic conditions. 
Persistent degradation in Jobos Bay was particularly evident in eastern Aguirre State Forest and along Camino del Indio. Eastern Aguirre State Forest contained a large area with only partial mangrove recovery, consistent with the combined influence of pre-existing environmental stressors and post-hurricane disturbance (Figure S7). Along Camino del Indio, illegal expansion into protected lands appears to have compounded storm-related canopy loss, with storm-damaged mangrove areas subsequently cleared or altered (Figure S8; Angel Dieppa, personal communication). These examples indicate that hurricane disturbance can interact with both chronic stress and coastal governance issues, reducing the likelihood of natural recovery and increasing the persistence of non-mangrove states.
Jobos Bay showed an anomalous reduction in mangrove coverage in the 2022 map (Figure 4). Because the 2022 mosaic was generated from imagery acquired during the first four months of the year (Table A1), this reduction cannot be attributed to Hurricane Fiona (September 2022). During plot sampling in 2022, numerous recently dead or stressed small stems (both small trees and sprouts associated with epicormic growth) were recorded in areas of mangroves that had evidence of post-hurricane recovery; mortality appeared to be associated with very dry field conditions observed at the affected sites (Ogurcak et al., in preparation). These observations are consistent with the exceptionally low precipitation recorded in 2021 (441 mm), at Jobos Bay NERR, which was the lowest annual precipitation in the 2001-2024 record (long-term annual mean of 955 ± 319 mm; station JOBJBMET; [56]).
The overall patterns observed here are consistent with studies showing that post-cyclone mangrove recovery depends not only on storm intensity but also on antecedent ecosystem condition, local hydrology, and geomorphic exposure. Asbridge et al. [57] reported broad post-cyclone recovery in Australian mangroves but identified localized areas of persistent damage, while Velázquez-Salazar et al. [3] found highly variable recovery after Hurricane Roslyn in Mexico, with limited recovery in previously degraded forests. These findings support the interpretation that lower recovery in Jobos Bay was likely influenced by the interaction between hurricane damage and pre-existing stressors rather than by storm exposure alone. Recovery may have been further constrained by weather extremes, including the exceptionally dry conditions recorded in 2021. 
Another important difference was that the 2017 post-storm NDVI in Rookery Bay exhibited a deeper and more prolonged functional decrease than in Jobos Bay. In polygons that were damaged in 2018 but subsequently recovered, NDVI began increasing in Jobos Bay shortly after Hurricane Maria, by November 2017. In Rookery Bay, NDVI continued to decline after Hurricane Irma and did not begin to recover until June 2018. A similar NDVI decline after the storm is shown in Lagomasino et al. [8] for southern Florida after hurricane Irma. By mid-2022, however, both reserves had returned to their climatological mean NDVI values and seasonal amplitudes.
The delayed NDVI recovery in southern Rookery Bay is consistent with the greater initial disturbance associated with its position immediately to the right of Hurricane Irma’s track. The large extent of Rookery Bay NERR provides a clear example of how hurricane impacts can vary according to position relative to the storm track. Hurricane Irma's eye crossed approximately the central portion of the reserve (Figure 1), placing the section analyzed in this study (southern Rookery Bay) on the right side of the storm track, where the strongest winds and storm surge occurred. This area experienced extensive canopy loss and a prolonged decline in NDVI following the storm. In contrast, areas left of the storm track (north section of Rookery Bay, not analyzed in this study) were less affected and retained a greater proportion of mangrove cover in the 2018 map [17]. This spatial asymmetry is consistent with previous observations that mangrove damage in Florida is typically greater on the right side of landfalling hurricanes than on the left side at comparable distances from the storm center [48]. By contrast, Jobos Bay was located 40 km from the left side of Hurricane Maria track, where wind and surge exposure were lower than near the storm’s right-front quadrant. Greater wind-driven canopy and structural damage in southern Rookery Bay could have prolonged the decline in NDVI by delaying leaf replacement and canopy rebuilding. However, higher surge and rainfall exposure at Rookery Bay in 2017 may also have contributed to the prolonged post-storm NDVI decline. For Hurricane Irma, Castañeda-Moya et al. [58] found that storm deposits extended up to 10-km inland, with vertical accretion several times higher than the long-term values, and Radabaugh et al. [52] found delayed mortality after the storm due to mud storm surge deposit. Storm surge and rainfall may prolong stress through salinity changes, sustained inundation, low soil oxygen availability, or altered sediment and nutrient conditions. Such effects could suppress leaf production or increase delayed mortality after the storm, even where the initial loss of canopy was predominantly wind-driven. 
4.7. Structural and functional recovery
The annual land-cover trajectories and NDVI time series capture complementary but limited dimensions of recovery, and it is worth stating precisely what each measures. Reclassification of a pixel to the mangrove class indicates that a canopy detectable at 2-m resolution and spectrally consistent with mangrove is present again; we refer to this as mapped canopy-class recovery. It does not measure stand height, basal area, biomass, age structure, species composition, or belowground condition, and a stand of resprouting stems or young saplings can satisfy it while remaining far from its pre-storm structure. NDVI measures canopy greenness; a return of NDVI to its climatological value and seasonal amplitude indicates spectral greenness recovery, which is a proxy for leaf area and canopy condition rather than a direct measurement of photosynthetic rate, carbon uptake, or ecosystem function. Neither indicator measures hydrologic stress, porewater salinity, soil redox state, or root damage, and the two indicators therefore bracket rather than establish ecological recovery. Demonstrating structural and functional recovery in the strict sense would require field or lidar measurements of stand structure and, for function, flux or physiological measurements; the structural plots surveyed at both reserves in 2022-2023 (Ogurcak et al., in preparation) provide the field basis for the first of these, and are consistent with the mapped pattern of canopy-class recovery accompanied by a continuing structural deficit.
NDVI indicated a two-steps recovery process in areas disturbed by the 2017 hurricanes that eventually recovered: after the sharp decline immediately after the storms there was an initial rapid rebound during the first post-storm year, followed by slower recovery through mid-2022 when seasonality was evident. The early rebound phase likely reflected leaf replacement, resprouting, and partial canopy closure, whereas the later phase may represent continued biomass accumulation and structural recovery [3,6]. The presence of an NDVI decline in less severely affected areas where pixels remained classified as mangrove in 2018, shows that functional disturbance extended beyond the area of mapped structural conversion. Conversely, the lack of an NDVI decline in 2022 indicates that the weaker local winds associated with Ian and Fiona did not produce a widespread canopy-greenness signal detectable at the spatial, temporal, and spectral resolution analyzed. Annual averages of vegetation indices have been used to monitor post-storm mangrove recovery [3,9,50,59]. However, the study of NDVI from single-date satellite overpasses, rather than from annual composites, allowed a detailed assessment of recovery timing in our study areas.
Combining very-high-resolution land-cover maps with dense NDVI time series allowed us to evaluate disturbance and recovery across complementary spatial and temporal scales. Land-cover trajectories of the annual maps identified pathways of structural recovery, whereas NDVI time series characterized changes in vegetation condition and recovery dynamics that preceded complete canopy restoration. By integrating these approaches, this framework provides managers with spatially explicit and temporally resolved information to support hydrologic restoration, shoreline stabilization, monitoring of persistent dieback, and prioritization of vulnerable sectors within protected estuaries. Because the workflow relies primarily on transferable remote-sensing datasets and reproducible classification approaches, it offers a scalable approach that can be adapted to other NERRs and mangrove regions exposed to repeated tropical cyclone disturbance, providing a template for combined structural-functional resilience assessment.
4.8. Methodological considerations and limitations
The combination of high-resolution land-cover maps, pixel-level trajectory analysis, stakeholder-informed corrections, and dense NDVI time series enabled a detailed assessment of post-storm mangrove recovery. Several limitations qualify that assessment, and they are grouped below by the part of the workflow they affect. Most were raised during peer review and several are addressed here only by narrowing the claims; the analyses that would resolve them are identified in the text.
Validation. The accuracy assessment available for the annual maps (Table A3) was designed to evaluate the map products, not the multi-year transitions analyzed here. It excludes hurricane-affected locations, aggregates all classes to mangrove versus non-mangrove, reuses the same reference set across years, and is not matched in time to each annual map. It therefore cannot establish the accuracy of the mangrove-to-soil, mangrove-to-water, and soil-to-mangrove transitions that drive the results, nor of the distinctions among soil, low vegetation, coastal water, inland water, and upland vegetation used in Tables 1-3. The trajectories should accordingly be read as provisional mapped-area patterns rather than as accuracy-adjusted area estimates, and the class-specific conclusions are correspondingly narrow. A change-stratified reference sample containing both stable and changed pixels, following Olofsson et al. [60], is the single most valuable addition that could be made to this work.
Uncertainty propagation. Each pixel sequence spans five or six maps, and classification error compounds across dates and classes. No uncertainty is propagated into the reported path proportions, so no confidence intervals accompany the mangrove cover values, transition percentages, recovery proportions, or dominant pathways. Differences between sites and between pathway classes are therefore described qualitatively rather than as precise ecological quantities (Section 4.1).
Spatial representativeness. The analyzed domains are the mangrove target areas, covering 14.0% of the 2017 mapped mangrove extent at Rookery Bay and 64.6% at Jobos Bay. The Rookery Bay domain is concentrated in the reserve’s southern and southeastern sector, which lay on the right side of Hurricane Irma’s track and sustained the greatest damage, so it is not a random sample of that reserve and its statistics are not reserve-wide. Conclusions for Rookery Bay should be understood as applying to southern Rookery Bay, and the cross-reserve comparison as a comparison between two unequally sampled domains.
Complete-case selection. Restricting the analysis to pixels with valid classifications in every mosaic removes cloud-obscured, shadowed, and post-processing-masked pixels, which are unlikely to be randomly distributed and are plausibly more frequent in the most degraded areas. The target areas are therefore biased toward consistently interpretable pixels, and the extent of persistent degradation is likely to be understated.
Temporal comparability and post-processing. Annual mosaics were built from imagery acquired in different seasons and from widely varying numbers of acquisitions (Table A1), which is a potential source of phenological and spectral inconsistency between years, and no map was produced for 2019, a key period for early recovery. WorldView coverage declined after 2020, particularly at Rookery Bay, where no imagery was available for 2024 and 2023 coverage was limited to the southern portion of the reserve. In addition, the temporal-prevalence post-processing uses the full multi-year record, so an annual map depends in part on later years and rare but genuine transitions can be removed along with rare misclassifications.
Positional uncertainty. Interannual co-registration was improved by manual alignment to shoreline vectors but was not quantified. At 2-m resolution, a residual offset of one pixel can create apparent soil-water-mangrove transitions along narrow coastal fringes, which is where the lowest recovery rates are reported; the coastal-versus-inland water contrast is the result most exposed to this.
Water-mask choices. The perennial-water persistence rule, the erosion radius, the 20 m coastal/inland threshold, and the manual removal of inland water bodies were set by inspection and are not calibrated. The coastal-versus-inland conclusion depends directly on them, and their sensitivity has not yet been tested.
NDVI analysis. The condition polygons were delineated from the same land-cover maps, and from the same later years, that define the recovery categories, so the NDVI series are descriptive rather than independent validation. Values are pooled spatial averages that conceal within-polygon heterogeneity, which matters most for the non-recovered class, where polygons contain mixtures of mangrove, soil, and water. Recovery-onset dates were identified by inspection rather than by a change-point criterion with confidence intervals. The closure normalization applied before spatial averaging cancels exactly at pixel level for a ratio index such as NDVI but does alter the polygon average by weighting pixels inversely to their across-band sum. At Jobos Bay the pre-2019 record is Landsat-only, and although the measured inter-sensor bias is small and of a sign that cannot explain the observed early rebound, no cross-sensor correction was applied.
Interpretation of the indicators. Reclassification to the mangrove class indicates mapped canopy-class recovery, and NDVI indicates spectral greenness recovery; neither establishes recovery of biomass, stand structure, species composition, or ecosystem function, and neither measures hydrologic or physiological stress (Section 4.7). Consequently, areas that appear spectrally recovered may still differ substantially from pre-storm conditions, while persistently low NDVI may reflect mixtures of sparse vegetation, exposed sediment, and shallow water rather than complete ecosystem collapse.
Causal inference. The contrast between the 2017 and 2022 storms rests on four site-storm combinations in which wind exposure is confounded with track, season, hydrology, geomorphology, antecedent condition, and image availability. It is consistent with wind being the dominant proximal control on detectable canopy disturbance, but it does not identify wind as the cause relative to rainfall and surge, and it cannot establish an ecological wind threshold.
Taken together, these limitations affect the precision of the reported quantities and the strength of the mechanistic attribution more than they affect the broad pattern of widespread canopy-class recovery alongside spatially clustered persistent degradation, which is supported by coherent spatial structure, field observation, and two independent remote-sensing indicators.
5. Conclusions
Annual 2-m resolution land-cover maps provided a basis for quantifying hurricane-related change in mapped mangrove canopy cover. Decision-tree classifications achieved consistently high performance across both reserves (overall and balanced accuracies of 0.92-0.93; MCC = 0.83), and confusion with upland vegetation was very low. These metrics were obtained for a binary mangrove/non-mangrove decision at reference locations outside the hurricane-affected areas, so they establish map quality where land cover was stable rather than the accuracy of the transitions analyzed here; the trajectories below are correspondingly reported as mapped-area patterns without uncertainty bounds.
The 2017 hurricanes caused extensive but incomplete canopy disturbance, whereas the 2022 storms produced little detectable canopy response. One year after the 2017 storms, 46% of the analyzed 2017 mangrove target area at Rookery Bay and 59% at Jobos Bay remained classified as mangrove, indicating substantial resistance in addition to subsequent recovery. These target areas comprise 14.0% and 64.6% of the 2017 mangrove extent mapped at each site, and the Rookery Bay domain is drawn from the reserve’s most damaged sector, so the values describe the analyzed areas rather than the reserves as a whole. In contrast, neither the annual land-cover maps nor the NDVI time series showed important disturbance following the 2022 storms. These contrasting responses are consistent with direct local exposure to hurricane-force winds in 2017 being the dominant proximal control on the widespread canopy disturbance detectable in this study, whereas extreme rainfall and storm surge without comparable wind exposure in 2022 did not produce a similar spectral response. With only four site-storm combinations, in which wind exposure is confounded with track, season, hydrology, geomorphology, and antecedent condition, this contrast does not identify wind as the cause relative to rainfall and surge, and it should not be read as a universal ecological wind threshold. The absence of a detectable 2022 signal likewise does not show that Ian and Fiona had no ecological effects, only that no widespread canopy response was detected at the resolution analyzed. 
Recovery of the mangrove class was substantial but spatially and temporally heterogeneous. Within the target areas, mangrove cover recovered to 90.5% of the 2017 value by 2023 at Rookery Bay and to 78.9% by 2023 and 83% by 2024 at Jobos Bay. Most disturbed pixels that transitioned to bare soil or low vegetation were again classified as mangrove in the 2020 map, that is within two to three years, although the absence of a 2019 map means the exact year of return cannot be resolved for these pixels; indicating that recovery was dominated by regrowth of surviving trees, resprouting, and/or advance regeneration rather than prolonged successional replacement. In contrast, pixels that transitioned to water had substantially lower recovery rates, particularly along exposed coastal fringes, consistent with persistent inundation, erosion, ponding, or other geomorphic and hydrologic changes that can limit recolonization. Persistent degradation was also associated with pre-existing hydrologic and anthropogenic stressors, demonstrating that antecedent conditions can constrain post-hurricane resilience.
NDVI provided a complementary measure of functional canopy response. Disturbed areas showed an initial rapid rebound during the first post-storm year followed by slower recovery through approximately mid-2022, consistent with progressive canopy rebuilding. NDVI also detected reductions in vegetation condition in areas that remained classified as mangrove in 2018, demonstrating that functional disturbance extended beyond areas of mapped structural conversion. Recovery timing differed between reserves: NDVI recovery began in Jobos Bay by November 2017 but was delayed until June 2018 in southern Rookery Bay, consistent with the greater disturbance associated with its position on the stronger right side of Hurricane Irma. Stronger surge and rainfall exposure at Rookery Bay may also have contributed to the prolonged response, and their effects cannot be separated from wind exposure with the available data.
The integration of very-high-resolution canopy-class trajectories with dense NDVI time series provides a transferable framework for tracking the structural and spectral dimensions of mangrove response to tropical cyclones, subject to the validation and uncertainty caveats set out in Section 4.8. The approach identifies both rapidly recovering forests and persistent degradation hotspots, providing spatially explicit information that can support long-term monitoring, hydrologic restoration, shoreline management, and conservation planning in mangrove ecosystems increasingly exposed to intense tropical storms.
