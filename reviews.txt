Reviewer 1
 This manuscript examines mangrove disturbance and recovery after Hurricanes Irma and Maria using annual WorldView-derived land-cover maps at approximately 2 m resolution and Sentinel-2 and Landsat NDVI series. The comparison of Rookery Bay and Jobos Bay, the explicit pixel trajectories, and the effort to distinguish rapidly recovering areas from persistent degradation hotspots are valuable. The scene inventory in Table A1, the class correspondence in Table A2, and the attempt to report more informative accuracy metrics than overall accuracy alone are also welcome. The observed decline in mapped mangrove cover in 2018 and the subsequent partial return of the mangrove class constitute a potentially useful contribution for reserve management.
I recommend Major Revision because the evidence currently does not support several central quantitative and mechanistic claims. The analyzed domains are smaller than reported, the validation design excludes the storm-affected locations that drive the change analysis, and binary map accuracy is used to justify multiyear transitions among unvalidated non-mangrove classes. Uneven scene timing, full-series post-processing, complete-case selection, and residual georegistration error are not propagated into trajectory uncertainty. The NDVI workflow and wind attribution also need substantial clarification and, in places, reanalysis. These concerns are remediable, but they affect the headline percentages and the interpretation of recovery.
Major scientific points
 1. Analysis domain, denominators, and a major arithmetic inconsistency
 Lines 386-391 state that the 2017 mangrove extent was 307.0 km2 in Rookery Bay and 9.6 km2 in Jobos Bay, and that the target areas represented 24.2 percent and 70.0 percent of those extents. Tables 1 and 2 instead give target-area baselines of 4,298.5 ha and 620.4 ha, equivalent to 42.985 km2 and 6.204 km2. These values represent 14.0 percent of 307.0 km2 and 64.6 percent of 9.6 km2, not 24.2 percent and 70.0 percent. Please identify which quantities are correct and propagate the correction through the Abstract, Highlights, Results, Discussion, Conclusions, figures, tables, and supplementary material.
 More importantly, the 46 to 90 percent and 59 to 83 percent trajectories are calculated only within pixels classified as mangrove in 2017 that also retain valid classifications in every subsequent mosaic. They are not estimates for the full pre-storm mangrove extent of either reserve. Replace "percent of pre-storm extent" with "percent of the analyzed 2017 mangrove target area" wherever applicable. Report the area excluded at each filtering step, by year and reason, map the excluded pixels, and evaluate whether the complete-case target area differs from the omitted mangrove area in exposure, location, initial disturbance, or land-cover composition.
2. The validation design is not aligned with the change and trajectory claims
 Section 2.2 states that validation points within hurricane-affected areas were excluded because their land cover changed after the storms. Those are precisely the locations for which the manuscript infers disturbance, soil exposure, water conversion, and recovery. The same validation set is reused across years within each region, supplemented by expert visual interpretation whose acquisition dates and sampling protocol are not reported. In addition, all map classes are aggregated to a binary mangrove versus non-mangrove assessment. Therefore, Table A3 cannot validate the distinctions among soil, low vegetation, coastal water, inland water, and upland vegetation that underpin Tables 1-3 and the mechanistic interpretation of the pathways. High accuracy in stable locations cannot establish low uncertainty in changed locations.
 A revision should include a temporally matched and spatially representative reference sample that contains both stable and changed pixels, preferably stratified by the principal transition types. Provide the sampling and response designs, reference-image dates, source of every label, full multiclass confusion matrices, class-specific user's and producer's accuracies, confidence intervals, and accuracy-adjusted area estimates. The validation should directly assess change categories or trajectory endpoints, following the principles in Olofsson et al. (2014, Remote Sensing of Environment 148, 42-57, doi:10.1016/j.rse.2014.02.015). If adequate historical reference data are unavailable, the authors should explicitly present the trajectories as provisional mapped-area patterns, remove claims of low uncertainty and robust ecological attribution, and substantially narrow the class-specific conclusions.
3. Temporal comparability, post-processing leakage, and positional uncertainty
 Table A1 shows large differences in the number and season of WorldView acquisitions. Rookery Bay mosaics range from 28 passes spanning January-August 2017 to a single February 2023 pass; the 2020 map spans March-July, the 2021 map combines September 2020-February 2021, and the 2022 map uses October-December imagery. Jobos Bay 2023 spans May-December, whereas 2024 uses January-February. A pixel-wise mode across such different temporal windows and observation counts is not automatically comparable, especially for phenology, water extent, shadows, and post-storm conditions. Provide scene identifiers and exact dates, valid-observation counts, spatial coverage, and per-pixel compositing support. Conduct a sensitivity analysis using a common seasonal window and, where feasible, comparable scene counts or single-scene subsets.
 The temporal-prevalence filtering also uses the full series to reclassify rare assignments and expert-defined masks to remove unlikely mangrove pixels. This can suppress genuine rare changes and makes an annual map depend on future years. Document the exact rules, thresholds, and operation order, then rerun the central trajectories without this full-series correction or with a strictly antecedent version. Finally, quantify interannual co-registration accuracy. At 2 m resolution, even a one-pixel offset can create apparent soil-water-mangrove transitions along narrow coastal fringes. Manual alignment is not an adequate uncertainty statement without control points, positional RMSE, and a displacement sensitivity test.
4. Propagate classification error into multiyear trajectories and recovery estimates
 Tables 1-3 treat each pixel sequence across five or six maps as exact, although error compounds across dates and classes. This is particularly consequential for low-frequency paths and for recurrent changes between water, soil, low vegetation, and mangrove. Please propagate annual class uncertainty into the reported path proportions, for example through Monte Carlo simulation using temporally appropriate confusion matrices together with a georegistration perturbation. Report confidence intervals for mangrove cover, transition percentages, recovery proportions, and the most common pathways. Without such propagation, differences between sites or pathway classes should not be presented as precise ecological quantities.
 Define resistance, recovery, first return to mangrove, final-year mangrove status, recurrent transition, and persistent degradation operationally. The absence of a 2019 map prevents precise claims that recovery occurred within two or three years. Reconcile the Rookery Bay water result reported as 48.0 percent recovery across paths with the 51.5 percent final mangrove value in Table 1, and distinguish clearly between all water, coastal water, and inland water denominators. Avoid "significant" unless an inferential test and uncertainty estimate are provided.
5. NDVI processing, sensor comparability, and circular selection of condition polygons
 The NDVI polygons are delineated from the same land-cover maps and later years used to classify areas as less impacted, recovered, or non-recovered. The NDVI curves are therefore useful descriptive case studies, but they are not independent validation of the recovery categories. Report the number, area, geometry, selection criteria, and pre-storm properties of all polygons, and provide uncertainty across polygons rather than only pooled area averages. Recovery timing should be estimated with a stated quantitative criterion or change-point method, with confidence intervals, rather than by visual comparison with a climatology derived from one selected condition class.
 The preprocessing is not reproducible. Identify the exact Cloud Score+ collection, QA band, and clear-pixel threshold. Reference 37 currently describes a different cloud product. For Landsat Collection 2 Level-2, state whether the 0.0000275 multiplicative factor and -0.2 offset were applied and how QA_PIXEL, QA_RADSAT, and SR_QA_AEROSOL were used. Explain how mixed-resolution Sentinel-2 bands were resampled before summing all bands. Because the workflow normalizes by the sum of all sensor bands before spatial averaging, it is sensor-dependent and is not identical in general to averaging standard pixel-level NDVI. Recalculate NDVI from properly scaled red and NIR reflectance at the pixel level before polygon aggregation, or demonstrate quantitatively that the current closure normalization does not change the results. Provide the Sentinel-Landsat paired sample size, pairing window, regression, uncertainty, and polygon-specific agreement metrics.
6. Wind attribution and the meaning of structural and functional recovery
 The contrast between two 2017 storms and two 2022 storms is observational and highly confounded by storm track, season, canopy state, hydrology, geomorphology, prior disturbance, and image availability. It does not identify wind as the primary causal driver relative to rainfall or surge, and four site-storm combinations cannot establish an ecological threshold. Please replace causal statements such as "primary driver", "natural experiment", and "threshold-dependent response" with language stating that the spectral pattern is consistent with stronger local wind exposure in 2017. If a stronger attribution is retained, provide site-specific wind, rainfall, surge, duration, and antecedent-condition variables together with an explicit comparative model and uncertainty.
 The constructs also require calibration. Reclassification to the mangrove class indicates recovery of detectable canopy cover, not recovery of forest structure, biomass, species composition, or ecosystem function. NDVI measures spectral greenness and canopy condition, not photosynthetic function directly. Use "mapped canopy-class recovery" and "spectral greenness recovery" throughout unless independent field or lidar evidence supports structural and functional recovery. Similarly, "no evident impacts in 2022" should be revised to "no widespread canopy signal detected at the spatial, temporal, and spectral resolution analyzed".
7. Water-mask sensitivity, reproducibility, and supplementary material
 The perennial-water procedure refers to pixels classified as water in at least six of seven years from 2014-2023, but the map inventory contains different numbers of mosaics and duplicate-year products by reserve. State the exact years and products used at each site. Justify and test the sensitivity of the six-year persistence rule, the five-pixel erosion radius, the 20 m coastal threshold, and the manual removal of inland water bodies. Because the coastal-versus-inland conclusion depends directly on these choices, report how recovery percentages change under reasonable alternatives.
 The data statement points to project products but does not provide the code and derived inputs required to reproduce this paper. Archive the analysis scripts, scene inventory, classifier thresholds, temporal-prevalence rules, expert masks, manual corrections, validation labels and provenance, target-area masks, pixel-path tables, NDVI polygon geometries, monthly observations, and environment information. Figures S1-S8 are repeatedly invoked but were not present in the review file available to me. Please include the complete supplement and add a formal Supplementary Materials statement identifying every item.

Reviewer 3
The manuscript presents a remote sensing-based assessment of mangrove disturbance and recovery following Hurricanes Irma and Maria in 2017 and Ian and Fiona in 2022 in Rookery Bay, Florida, and Jobos Bay, Puerto Rico. The authors combine high-resolution WorldView land-cover maps with higher-temporal-resolution NDVI time series from Sentinel-2 and Landsat. The topic is interesting and relevant to remote sensing and coastal ecosystem monitoring, and the combination of structural and spectral information is potentially useful. However, I have several concerns about the current analysis and interpretation. In particular, some of the main conclusions appear stronger than what can be fully supported by the study design and available data. I therefore recommend Major Revision. 
The manuscript reports high classification accuracy, with overall accuracy around 0.92–0.93 and good F1/MCC values. However, validation points within hurricane-affected areas were excluded because land cover had changed after the storms. This creates an important problem because the disturbed areas are exactly the areas used for the main trajectory analysis. 
Thus, the reported high classification accuracy mainly demonstrates classification performance in areas that were not strongly affected, but it does not necessarily prove that transitions such as mangrove → soil, mangrove → water, or soil → mangrove are accurately detected in the disturbed regions. This is particularly important because the major ecological conclusions are based on these pixel-wise trajectories. 
The authors should provide some independent validation of representative disturbed areas, for example using post-storm field observations, historical very-high-resolution imagery, or other independent reference data. At minimum, the manuscript should clearly discuss this limitation and avoid implying that the high overall accuracy automatically guarantees reliable disturbance and recovery trajectories. 
Only 24.2% of the 2017 mangrove area in Rookery Bay was included in the complete trajectory analysis, compared with 70.0% in Jobos Bay. The manuscript mentions limited WorldView coverage, but the implications of this problem are not sufficiently discussed. 
This is more than a simple missing-data issue. The retained 24.2% is not necessarily a random sample of the entire Rookery Bay mangrove ecosystem. It is concentrated in the southern/southeastern portion, which also experienced substantial storm damage. Therefore, the reported recovery from 46% in 2018 to 90% in 2023 may not be representative of Rookery Bay as a whole.
This also makes the comparison between Rookery Bay and Jobos Bay somewhat problematic because the spatial coverage and sampling design are very different. The authors should better evaluate the representativeness of the retained area, preferably by comparing included and excluded areas using available information such as storm exposure, distance to coast, elevation, pre-storm vegetation condition, or other environmental characteristics. Otherwise, the conclusions should be explicitly limited to the analyzed southern Rookery Bay area. 
The paper argues that the strong contrast between 2017 and 2022 indicates that local hurricane-force wind was the primary driver of the widespread mangrove disturbance. I think this conclusion is somewhat too strong given the current study design. 
Wind speed, storm surge, rainfall, storm track, antecedent ecosystem condition, hydrology, geomorphology, and forest structure all differed between the storms and cannot be independently separated in the present analysis. The manuscript itself acknowledges that these factors may interact. 
Therefore, the absence of a detectable NDVI or land-cover response after Ian and Fiona should not be interpreted as evidence that those storms had no important ecological effects. They may have caused hydrological, physiological, or delayed impacts that were not detectable by the remote sensing indicators used here. 
The manuscript normalizes each spectral band by dividing it by the sum of reflectance across all bands before calculating NDVI. This is an important processing step, but the rationale and potential influence on the final NDVI results are not sufficiently demonstrated. 
The authors should explain more clearly why this normalization is necessary after using surface-reflectance products and what specific source of variation it is intended to remove. More importantly, a sensitivity analysis should be considered by comparing NDVI derived from the original surface reflectance with NDVI derived after normalization. 
Because the study relies heavily on small changes in NDVI during recovery, it is important to demonstrate that the normalization does not introduce artificial trends or modify the timing and magnitude of recovery. 
Using Landsat to extend the Jobos Bay time series before Sentinel-2 became available is reasonable, but the current cross-sensor treatment is not fully convincing. The authors report relatively small inter-sensor differences during 2019–2025 and conclude that the two datasets are comparable. However, even a bias of 0.03–0.05 NDVI can become relevant when evaluating gradual recovery, especially when the recovery signal is much smaller than the initial hurricane-induced decline. 
The authors should provide more details on how Landsat and Sentinel-2 observations were matched during the overlap period and whether a cross-sensor correction was applied. A useful additional analysis would be to show the recovery trajectories separately for Landsat and Sentinel-2 and demonstrate that the major conclusions are unchanged. Otherwise, the interpretation of the combined time series should be more cautious. 
The NDVI analysis mainly uses spatially averaged values over polygons. Although this makes the time series easy to interpret, it may conceal considerable internal heterogeneity. This is particularly important for the non-recovered areas, where the polygons may contain mixtures of mangrove, soil, and water. 
The manuscript sometimes uses “recovery” in a broad ecological sense, although the actual measurements mainly represent recovery of mapped mangrove cover or NDVI. Return to the mangrove class does not necessarily indicate recovery of biomass, canopy height, species composition, forest structure, or below-ground ecological function. Likewise, NDVI should not be treated as a direct measurement of photosynthetic function.
 
Reviewer 4
The paper “Mangrove disturbance and recovery following Hurricanes Irma 2 and Maria (2017) in Florida and Puerto Rico”. This manuscript focuses on analyzing mangrove disturbance and recovery following Hurricanes Irma and Maria in Rookery Bay, Southwest Florida, and Jobos Bay, Puerto Rico. The study uses annual land cover maps derived from multi-temporal WorldView imagery with decision tree, and Sentinel-2 and Landsat-8/9 imagery for NDVI time-series analysis. The main advantage of this study is the detailed analysis of mangrove maps at 2 m spatial resolution for the period 2017-2024 which are highly useful for evaluating the spatial patterns of mangrove disturbance and recovery following hurricane events. In addition, the high-temporal analysis of Sentinel-2 and Landsat-8/9 NDVI further strengthens the assessment of the responses of disturbed and recovering mangrove forests.
Major concerns: Overall, the methodology and findings are scientifically sound. However, the manuscript does not discuss the possibility of mangrove gain or expansion during 2017-2024. For example, areas classified as bare soil or other non-mangrove classes before the hurricanes may have transitioned to mangrove by 2024. In fact, Figure A3, S7, and S8 indicates areas of mangrove gain (shown in blue), but these changes are not clearly discussed in the manuscript. The authors should assess whether such gains occurred using the annual land-cover maps and NDVI time series, and clearly distinguish newly established mangroves from the recovery of pre-existing mangroves. If no substantial mangrove gain occurred, this should also be explicitly stated and discussed.
In addition, I have several comments and suggestions regarding the manuscript:
Introduction Section. Page 3 (Ln 91-110). Current Introduction focuses primarily on previous studies conducted in Florida and Puerto Rico. The authors should broaden the literature review by including relevant studies on mangrove disturbance and recovery from other regions. In addition, previous studies that have employed NDVI time-series analysis to characterize the spectral responses should also be discussed. This will help clarify the advantages of the current study.
Method Section. Page 4 (Section 2.2). It is unclear whether the annual land cover maps derived from WorldView imagery and the decision tree algorithm were produced as part of the current study or were existing products generated by the previous NERRS-MCC project. In this section, the authors describe the processing steps used to produce the annual land-cover maps, but they also state that “NERRS-MCC produced a time series of 2-m annual land-cover maps spanning 2014-2024 for Rookery Bay and Jobos Bay NERRs and the surrounding ecosystems extending beyond the reserve boundaries.” The authors should clarify the source of these land-cover maps and clearly distinguish which processing and products were generated in the present study and which were obtained from previous work.
Page 6 (Ln 228). In addition to the overall accuracy metrics reported in Table A3, class-specific accuracy metrics should also be provided (can be add in supplementary materials).
Results Section. Page 8 (Ln 322). Classification accuracy report. Compared to the Jobos Bay that relatively more stable, for the Rookery Bay the ACC is relatively high (0.93), while the overall F1 (0.87) and precision (PPV = 0.82) are noticeably lower. This discrepancy is also apparent for individual years; for example, in 2018, ACC reaches 0.92, whereas the F1 score and PPV are only 0.84 and 0.78, respectively. The lower precision indicates that false-positive mangrove classifications may still be substantial. This finding needs to be more discussed.
Discussion Section. Current discussion is generally well structured. However, it lacks sufficient explanation of the factors driving the spatially heterogeneous patterns of mangrove recovery and persistent loss. For example, Section 4.3 identifies two dominant recovery pathways: transitions from bare soil to mangrove and from mixed low-relief vegetation to mangrove, but does not clearly explain why some areas recovered while others remained disturbed. The authors should discuss potential controlling factors and, if possible support this with auxiliary data such as distance to the coastline, soil conditions, elevation, hydrological setting, or distance from the hurricane track.
Minor comment:
Add scale bar to each map presented in the manuscript (e.g., Figures 1A, 2, and 2A) to improve spatial interpretation and consistency across figures.
